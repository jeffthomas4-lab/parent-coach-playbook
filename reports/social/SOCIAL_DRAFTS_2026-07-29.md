## 6. The fairness test for parent-coaches

**Source:** evergreen resurface, originally published 2026-05-13 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/drive-home/coaching-your-own-kid-fairly/
**Format:** carousel outline (7 slides)

**Core copy / slide outline:**
> **Slide 1 (cover):** The fairness test for parent-coaches. "I'm harder on my own kid" doesn't actually answer the question.
> **Slide 2:** There are two ways to be unfair, not one. Overcorrection: louder and longer with your own kid than with anyone else's. Undercorrection: skipping the correction because you don't want them singled out.
> **Slide 3:** Most parent-coaches drift into one of these without knowing which. The team usually notices before you do.
> **Slide 4:** The test: ask your assistant coach, not your spouse, not your kid. "Am I coaching my kid the same as the others? If not, which way am I off?" A good assistant knows inside ten seconds.
> **Slide 5:** The two-week log. Track three corrections per practice: who got corrected, what words, how long it took. Your own kid showing up more than they should means overcorrecting. Showing up less means undercorrecting.
> **Slide 6:** The fix is small once you know the direction. Same tone, same length, same number of words for your kid as for everyone else. The team notices the change inside three practices.
> **Slide 7 (close):** Aim for predictable, not perfectly fair. The other parents being able to predict your calls solves the perception problem, not more hand-wringing about whether you're hard enough on your own kid.

## 7. Ask about development, not minutes

**Source:** evergreen resurface, originally published 2026-06-20 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/drive-there/how-to-talk-to-the-coach-about-playing-time/

**Format:** link post

**Core copy:**
> Before you talk to the coach about playing time, figure out who actually has the problem. Is your kid upset about it, or are you? Those need different responses, and if your kid is mostly fine, the meeting with the coach probably shouldn't happen.
>
> When it is worth having: request a real meeting, don't ambush at the parking lot after a game. A text that reads "Would you have ten minutes this week to talk about my kid's development?" gets a different response than catching a coach mid-handshake line.
>
> Go in with questions, not demands. "What does he need to work on to earn more time?" is a different question than "why isn't he playing more?" and it's the one that keeps a coach honest instead of putting them on defense.
>
> A good meeting ends with a clear development target your kid can control. It doesn't end with a promise of more minutes. Those are two different outcomes, and only one of them is realistic.

**Platform tags:**
- **Facebook:** full copy, works well shared into team-parent groups mid-season when this comes up most.
- **X:** trim to the hook plus link: "Before you talk to the coach about playing time: figure out who actually has the problem, your kid or you. Then ask 'what does he need to work on,' not 'why isn't he playing more.' One keeps a coach honest. The other puts him on defense." + link.
- **Instagram caption:** full copy, hashtags #playingtime #sportsparents #youthcoaching.
- **Pinterest pin description:** "How to talk to your kid's coach about playing time without damaging the relationship. From Parent Coach Desk."
