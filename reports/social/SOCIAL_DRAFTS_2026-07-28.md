## 1. Backward warmup throws are going away

**Source:** newest published, 2026-07-23 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/news/nfhs-track-field-throwing-warmup-safety-2027/
**Format:** link post

**Core copy:**
> Starting with the 2027 season, NFHS is banning backward warmup throws in shot put and discus.
>
> Right now a lot of throwers still warm up the old way: standing on the toe board, throwing back over their head, away from the sector. Nobody in that motion can see where the implement is landing.
>
> The new rule makes every warmup throw face the sector from inside the circle, the same direction as competition. It doesn't take effect until next spring, but throwing coaches can start teaching the new form this fall.
>
> If your kid throws, ask their coach now whether practice already uses the new form or the old one.

## 3. Know which dancer you have

> Before you write the YAGP entry-fee check, know which dancer you have.
>
> YAGP is a scholarship and scouting event first, a competition second. Faculty from major schools and companies attend to hand out summer intensive scholarships, not just medals.

## 5. What a real off-season strength program looks like

**Source:** evergreen resurface, originally published 2026-02-15 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/body/off-season-conditioning-safety/
**Format:** checklist / short list post

**Core copy:**
> Thinking about a strength and conditioning program for your kid this off-season? Here's what separates the real ones from the ones to skip.
>
> **Worth paying for:**
> - Supervised by a credentialed strength coach (CSCS or similar)
> - Progressive: technique first, load added slowly
> - Built around 8-10 hours of sleep and 1-2 rest days a week
> - Runs $50-150 a session, or $400-800 a month at a quality facility
>
> **Walk away from:**
> - Unsupervised CrossFit-style workouts copied from Instagram
> - Sprint or distance volume with nothing to do with your kid's actual sport
> - Any program built on "more training is automatically better"
>
> A kid-led session in the school weight room with credentialed staff supervising is often free, and does the same job as a paid program done right.

**Platform tags:**

## 6. The real numbers behind varsity soccer tryouts

**Source:** evergreen resurface, originally published 2026-07-06 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/drive-there/varsity-soccer-tryouts-the-real-odds/
**Format:** carousel outline (8 slides)

**Core copy / slide outline:**
> **Slide 1 (cover):** The real numbers behind varsity soccer tryouts, before your kid walks in.
> **Slide 2:** 60-70 kids show up for about 35 total spots across JV and varsity at a big program. Do that math before day one, not after the list goes up.
> **Slide 3:** Varsity carries 18-22. JV carries another 18-22. Unlike football or track, soccer cuts.
> **Slide 4:** The coach already has most of the roster in their head from club season film and talking to club coaches. Tryout week confirms it and settles the last four or five spots.
> **Slide 5:** 11 starting jobs exist. A freshman who wins one in August can lose it by October if a senior takes it back in practice.
> **Slide 6:** Club reputation buys a longer look in week one. It doesn't buy a starting spot if a kid can't translate club speed of play to an 11v11 high school game.
> **Slide 7:** What actually helps before tryouts: offseason pickup runs if the program has them, daily touches on a size-5 ball all summer, knowing your position group's depth before day one.
> **Slide 8 (close):** A cut sophomore who keeps training and tries again junior year makes varsity more often than families expect. One August afternoon isn't the whole story.

**Platform tags:**
- **Instagram:** native 8-slide carousel as outlined. Caption repeats the slide-1 hook, hashtags #varsitysoccer #tryouts #highschoolsoccer.

## 7. The first thing a coach should know

**Source:** evergreen resurface, originally published 2026-06-11 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/team-parent/10-things-kids-wish-coaches-knew/
**Format:** link post

**Core copy:**
> If a twelve-year-old could hand your team's coach a note, the first line would be: use my name.
>
> Not just on the roster. In practice, on the sideline, after a good rep or a bad one. "Good rep" lands one way. "Good rep, Tyler" lands completely differently, because it tells the kid the comment was actually for them.
>
> Most coaches use names constantly in week one, then drift to pointing and "good job everyone" once the roster settles in. Kids notice exactly when that happens.
>
> That's one of ten things kids say they actually want from a coach, none of which cost a practice plan change. The rest of the list is worth five minutes before your next practice.
