## 1. Most helmet damage happens at the sink, not the field

**Source:** newest published, 2026-08-03 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/team-parent/helmet-care-without-wrecking-it/
**Format:** single tip card

**Core copy:**
> Most helmet damage doesn't happen during the season. It happens in the garage, at the sink.
>
> Clean it with mild, non-citrus dish soap and warm water only. A rag or an old toothbrush handles the crevices. That's the whole job.
>
> Skip commercial helmet cleaners, car wax, glass cleaner, bleach, and solvents. Schutt and Riddell both say the same thing: those break down the shell and the foam, and they can void the warranty.
>
> Don't submerge it. Water gets past the pads, and foam that stays wet doesn't dry right before the next practice.
>
> A clean helmet still isn't a certified one by itself. NOCSAE and NAERA recommend reconditioning and recertifying a helmet every one to two years. Most helmets can be recertified up to about ten years old. After that, it gets replaced, not cleaned again.

## 2. What actually happens at a hockey tryout

**Source:** seasonal (SEASONAL_PLAN_2026-08.md social queue, hockey), originally published 2026-06-11 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/drive-there/youth-hockey-tryouts-what-to-expect/
**Format:** carousel outline (7 slides)

**Core copy / slide outline:**
> **Slide 1 (cover):** Hockey tryouts land in the next few weeks for a lot of 11 and 12-year-olds. Here's what actually happens on the ice.
> **Slide 2:** Session one is skating only. Coaches watch edges, crossovers, backward skating, and how a kid recovers when they lose an edge. Puck skills come later.
> **Slide 3:** Session two is puck handling, passing, and shooting circuits. Kids rotate through drills fast. One rep, then rotate. No time to fix a mistake or show it again.
> **Slide 4:** Session three, if there is one, is compete: small-area games, three-on-three, sometimes a scrimmage. This is where coaches see if a kid reads the play and competes under pressure.
> **Slide 5:** What actually moves a kid up: skating plus compete, not the smoothest stickhandling. Programs can teach puck skills on a timeline. They can't easily teach a kid to compete.
> **Slide 6:** Your job in the stands: watch the ice, stay out of the lobby conversations, say nothing to other parents about how it's going.
> **Slide 7 (close):** The drive home line that works: "That looked hard and you kept competing." Results usually come within a week, by email or a posted list at the rink.

## 3. The 8-10 cleat conversation, before you buy

**Source:** seasonal (SEASONAL_PLAN_2026-08.md social queue, soccer/cross-country gear window), originally published 2026-01-19 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/drive-there/8-10-cleat-conversation/
**Format:** checklist / list post

**Core copy:**
> Most eight-year-olds don't need cleats yet. Before you buy a pair, run this list.
>
> - Watch them play first. Indoor court or hard-packed dirt: flat shoes are fine. Grass or turf where they're slipping on cuts and starts: that's when cleats earn their keep.
> - Wait until after the first month of the season. Let them play in running shoes and see if they actually slip before you spend anything.
> - If you buy, go molded firm-ground with rubber studs, not metal. Skip soft-ground cleats: those are the long screw-in studs for mud, most youth leagues don't allow them, and most fields never get muddy enough to need them.
> - Size up half a size from their normal shoe and have them try the pair on wearing the socks they'll actually wear to play. If they say tight, they're too tight.
> - Budget forty to sixty dollars. That's the real range for this age. Anything above that is the brand name, not the shoe.
>
> They'll outgrow these in one season. That's not a bad purchase. That's an eight-year-old growing.

**Platform tags:**

## 4. Do you have a heat plan, or does someone decide in the moment?

**Source:** evergreen resurface, originally published 2026-05-13 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/game/heat-illness-when-to-pull-them-out/
**Format:** discussion-question post

**Core copy:**
> It's still August. The field is 90-plus most afternoons. Your kid finishes warmup and looks pale.
>
> Watch for headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, or skin that looks different from how they normally look after running. A kid who stops sweating or stumbles isn't toughing it out.
>
> See two of those signs at once and you don't ask the coach first. Pull the kid to shade, water, and a cooler surface. Cool wet towels on the neck and groin, cold water in sips not gulps, per CDC and American Academy of Pediatrics guidance.
>
> If a kid stops sweating and seems confused or out of it, that's a 911 call, not a drive to urgent care.
>
> Do you have a heat plan for this season, or does someone just decide in the moment?

## 5. The used-gear math nobody runs

**Source:** evergreen resurface, originally published 2026-01-22, updated 2026-08-02 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/drive-there/used-vs-new-gear-the-actual-math/
**Format:** short thread (5 posts)

**Core copy:**
> 1/ The cheapest listing isn't always the cheapest season. Before you buy used gear, run the actual math instead of trusting the discount sticker.
>
> 2/ Add it up: purchase price, plus required inspection or reconditioning, plus replacement parts, plus shipping. Subtract realistic resale value. That's your net cost.
>
> 3/ Divide net cost by the number of seasons the kid can safely and legally use it. A $40 used helmet with no way to verify its certification isn't a deal. It's not eligible for the comparison at all.
>
> 4/ Some costs are gates, not tradeoffs. Unverifiable helmet history, an expired bat certification, a bike helmet with unknown impact history: leave those out of the math entirely, they don't get compared.
>
> 5/ New wins when a kid will use it for several seasons or the warranty has real value. Used wins when fit is confirmed and a growing kid will outgrow it before wearing it out. Run the math after safety clears, not instead of it.

## 6. The meeting that prevents week-four blowups

**Source:** evergreen resurface, originally published 2026-06-13 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/team-parent/how-to-run-a-preseason-parent-meeting/
**Format:** link post

**Core copy:**
> Most coaches skip the preseason parent meeting, or cover schedules and equipment in twenty minutes and call it done. Then week four hits with three angry email threads and a parent who's been stewing about playing time since game two.
>
> One meeting up front prevents most of it. Thirty to forty minutes, before the first practice: who you are as a coach, how you want to be reached, how you decide who plays and how much, and what you actually expect on the sideline.
>
> Say the playing-time system out loud, even the parts that make you nervous. Parents who know the system can live with decisions they disagree with. Parents who don't turn every substitution into evidence of unfairness.
>
> Send a short follow-up email after: three or four key points, not a transcript. That's the document you point back to when something comes up in October.

**Platform tags:**

## 7. Flag football registration is closing. Here's the whole season.

**Source:** seasonal (SEASONAL_PLAN_2026-08.md flagged content gap, only dedicated flag football asset), originally published 2026-06-10 — https://parentcoachdesk.com/drive-there/first-season-of-flag-football/
**Format:** link post

**Core copy:**
> Flag football registration is closing in a lot of leagues right now, if it hasn't already. Here's the whole first season in one place.
>
> Gear is three items: a mouthguard, cleats you probably already own, and a water bottle. The league supplies the flag belts and usually the jerseys. Total spend is about fifty dollars.
>
> Games run 5v5 or 7v7 on a shortened field. No tackling, no blocking in most formats, pull the flag and the play is dead. Seasons run six to ten weeks.
>
> Your job on the sideline: cheer loudly, coach never. One sentence after the game does it: "I love watching you play."
>
> If a coach says your kid should move up to tackle after one good season, that's a conversation on your timeline, not theirs.
