Eli asks for extra reps after every practice, runs the route again after the whistle blows, wants the playbook emailed to him instead of just handed out. He shows up twenty minutes early to throw with anyone who'll catch.

That's not a problem. That's a gift, and most coaches would trade for it.

The problem shows up in the other nine kids on the field, the ones who came to play with their friends and go home on time. When Eli runs a fourth rep while they're still catching their breath from the third, or corrects a teammate's footwork mid-drill before the coach gets to it, the gift turns into a tax the rest of the team pays.

## Practice serves the team. Hunger gets fed somewhere else.

The fix isn't slowing Eli down. The fix is building him a second track that doesn't run through shared practice time.

Give the hungry kid extra reps before practice starts, not during it. Fifteen minutes at 5:30 instead of 5:45, throwing with an assistant coach or a parent volunteer who's willing to show up early works fine. Send the playbook home and take his questions over text instead of in the huddle.

Add a Tuesday film session after the team session ends, twenty minutes, just him and a coach, breaking down the routes he ran that week.

None of that touches the sixty minutes the rest of the roster signed up for. All of it feeds what Eli actually wants.

## Watch what he does with the extra

A kid who takes the second track and settles down in the shared one is developing exactly the way you want. A kid who takes the second track and still corrects teammates, still checks out visibly during reps beneath his level, still treats team time like a warm-up for his real practice, has a different problem on his hands.

The extra work didn't fix it. It gave him a new stage to run it on, which is the cue for a direct conversation instead of another rep.

## The conversation with the hungry kid

Say the structure out loud instead of letting him guess at it. Practice belongs to the team. The extra time is his, and he has to ask for it instead of building it himself in the middle of a drill everyone else is running.

Tell him straight: "You get more of my time than anyone else on this roster. That's not payment for running this practice. It's an investment in you getting better, and those are two different things you're currently treating as one."

Most hungry kids take that deal without blinking, because they never actually wanted to slow the team down. They wanted more, and nobody had told them where to put it.

## What the rest of the roster notices

The team doesn't need Eli to want less, just to stop absorbing the cost of him wanting more. Move the extra reps to 5:30 and the extra film to Tuesday, and practice goes back to being sixty minutes that belong to everyone in the huddle, not sixty minutes plus whatever Eli decided to add on top of it.

Two seasons in, Eli is still the first kid at the field and the last one to leave. The rest of the team stopped noticing, because it stopped happening on their time.

### A note on the ones who say no

Some hungry kids reject the second track. They want the attention in front of teammates, not before practice where nobody sees it. That's not about development anymore, and it's worth naming what the attention is actually for before you build him a single extra rep.
