My son missed the same block three times in one inside-run period this spring. Same gap, same defender, same result. Thirteen other kids stood on the line waiting for the whistle to move on.

Every rep he took landed the same way. My stomach knotted watching it. The rest of the group started drifting: checking cleats, glancing toward the parking lot, done paying attention to a drill that had turned into one kid's audition.

## Two ways this goes wrong

Ride him harder than anyone else on the field to prove you're not playing favorites, and the whole practice tightens around his mistakes. Wave him through easy reps instead, and the other thirteen clock it by the second rep. Kids track fairness better than most adults in the building.

Neither move is really about him by that point. Something else has taken over: how I look, standing next to my own kid, in front of everyone.

## Practice can't run through one kid's day

Fix the structure before you try to fix the moment. Rotate blocking assignments every two reps.

No single gap sits on one lineman's shoulders for the whole period. Split fourteen kids into three stations of four or five instead of one line watching a single rep unfold.

A kid struggling at right guard gets three fresh chances at technique work, spread across the period, not one long audition in front of the whole team.

None of that fixes my son's footwork. Not by itself. It buys him room to work on the footwork without thirteen kids grading every rep he takes.

## Your face is the scoreboard the team actually reads

Thirteen kids clock my jaw before they clock the missed block. Every time. A visible sigh when my own kid blows an assignment reads louder to the group than the assignment itself does, and it sets the room's temperature faster than anything on the play sheet.

I started counting to three before saying anything to any lineman who missed a block. Same count. Same tone, whoever's jersey it is.

My son gets the exact correction the kid next to him gets: no shorter fuse, no longer one.

## Save the real conversation for the car

The correction my son actually needs is specific: hand placement, first step, eyes on the near number before the snap. That conversation happens on the drive home or at the kitchen table. It doesn't happen in front of the line.

On the field he gets the same rep count and the same tone as every other lineman. No extra grace. No extra heat either.

## The sideline reads this differently than the huddle does

Other parents watch a coach's kid struggle and expect one of two things. Either you bench him to prove you're fair, or you protect him to prove you're not. Both readings assume the whole decision is about optics.

I stopped explaining myself to the fence. The only person who gets an explanation for a coaching call is the kid it affects, not the parents standing behind the rope with an opinion about my son's footwork.

That took longer to learn than the drill fix did. Coaching your own kid means somebody is always narrating your motives back to you, and most of that narration has nothing to do with what actually happened on the play.

Three weeks later he still missed some reps. So did the kid at left tackle. Practice stopped being about either of them and went back to being fourteen guys working a drill.
