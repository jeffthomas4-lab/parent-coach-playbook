Cast list goes up Friday. Your kid's name isn't on it. Or it is, third from the bottom, one line buried in a scene nobody in the audience will remember.

The car ride home turns into a referendum on whether theater is even worth doing anymore. Nobody mentions the other list. It goes up the same day, in the same hallway, and nobody auditions for a single job on it: the crew sign-up sheet.

## Crew runs the show too

Set, costumes, lights, sound, stage management. Actors get maybe fifteen slots out of forty kids who tried out for them. Crew has room for all forty and then some, because a real production needs more hands backstage than it needs bodies in front of the curtain.

Stage management stands apart from the rest. It's the closest thing theater has to a leadership job, the one person besides the director who tracks the whole show cold, calling cues and running rehearsal when the director steps out. Some programs treat it as a multi-year track, not a one-show tryout, and hand it to a kid who's already logged a season on headset.

## Getting on it

Ask the director directly, the same day the cast list posts: is there a crew sign-up, and where. A few programs tape it right next to the cast list. Others only say it out loud in the room, once, and never write it down anywhere a parent would think to look.

Most crew jobs skip the audition and the interview both. A kid tells the technical director which department interests them, and that's the whole process. Stage management can work differently.

A handful of programs interview for it, or ask a returning stage manager who they'd trust with the job next year. That job carries real weight once a show is running, and directors treat the hire that way.

Move fast. Crew rosters fill inside a week, lights and sound first since every theater kid already knows those look good on a resume. Wait two weeks, and set construction might be the only door still open.

## What the jobs actually are

Set crew builds and paints, and the hammering starts weeks before a single actor blocks a scene. Tools, plywood, a finished thing still standing at strike, is why a kid who likes working with their hands belongs here.

Costume crew fits, alters, and manages the ninety-second quick change in the wings. The work never really stops once the show opens. Lights and sound crews run actual professional-grade booth equipment, a specific skill most kids never touch anywhere else in a school building, and stage management sits in every rehearsal from the first table read on, tracking every blocking note and calling every cue once the curtain finally goes up.

None of it is a consolation prize. A kid who wanted the lead and landed on set construction instead is doing different work. Not smaller work.

## When the real question is quitting

A kid who wanted the stage and didn't get it needs a reason to stay, not a speech about how crew matters. Point them at whichever department sits closest to what drew them to theater in the first place. Running the light board still shapes what the audience sees, just from a different chair, in the dark, at the back of the house.

A kid who never wanted to perform has the easier answer. Crew is where they belonged from the first day of auditions, and skipping the whole audition circus is a fine way to walk straight into a production.

Either way, skip "settle for crew." Say the other sentence instead: here's a job that matters, and it starts this week, if you want it.
