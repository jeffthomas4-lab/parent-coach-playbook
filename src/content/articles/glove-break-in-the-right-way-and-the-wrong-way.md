A brand-new glove closes like a book with the spine still glued shut. Every kid wants it game-ready in a weekend, and most of the shortcuts for getting there fast put the leather at risk.

## What actually works

Pour warm water, not boiling, into the palm and heel with the fingers pointed up so it doesn't pool in the finger stalls. Wilson's own glove craftsmen use that step to relax the leather before shaping it. Work the two hinge points at the heel by hand, twisting the thumb side and pinky side back and forth until the glove starts folding where you want it to close.

A wooden mallet does the rest. Pound the outside of the thumb and pinky above the wrist opening to set the break points, then pound and roll inside the pocket to shape it deep or shallow, whichever your kid's position calls for.

## Skip the oven

Rawlings, the company that actually builds these gloves, tells its own customers not to steam, boil, bake, or otherwise heat a glove to break it in. Petroleum jelly is on the same no list. Both damage leather and can void the warranty, and neither speeds up what a mallet and warm water already do safely.

A light coat of leather conditioner is fine on stiff premium leather. Heavy is the mistake. Soak a glove in oil and it goes soft in a way that won't hold a pocket or last a season.

## Forming the pocket

Once the glove closes without a fight, put a ball in the pocket, fold the glove around it, and wrap it shut with rubber bands or a glove strap. Let it sit overnight. The shape holds better than anything a mallet does alone.

Then play catch. A lot of it. The oils from a kid's hand keep softening the lining, and the ball keeps shaping the pocket every time the glove closes around it. Nothing breaks in a glove faster than actual use.

## Keep it that way

Check the laces every six months and replace them before they snap mid-game, not after. Store the glove with a ball still in the pocket, cool and dry, never on a car dashboard. A glove broken in this way outlasts the kid who's using it.
