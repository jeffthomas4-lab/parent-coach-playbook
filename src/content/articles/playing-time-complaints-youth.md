Your kid gets in the car after a game where they played six minutes, and the first words are "Coach hates me." The urge is to email the coach before you pull out of the lot. Don't. Listen first and wait a day or two before you ask for anything.

That order protects two relationships at once: yours with your child, and your child's with the coach. And it beats a parking-lot talk.

## The 72-hour plan

**Day one: listen and watch.** Let your child talk without correcting them. Then watch the next practice or game with a notebook mindset: arrival time, effort in drills, what the coach says to your kid and how.

**Day two: wait and ask.** Sleep on it. Then ask your child a curious question, like "Why do you think the coach is making this choice?" Skip "That's not fair."

**Day three: decide if you still need a meeting.** If the pattern holds past one rough game, ask for one. Keep a simple note through all three days: the date, what you saw, and what your child said.

## What to say to your child, by age

Elementary school: name the feeling. "I saw you were sad after the game. What was the hardest part?" At 7 or 8, being heard matters more than any plan.

Middle school: add one problem to solve. "What do you think the coach wants to see? Is there one thing you can work on this week?" Turn vague feedback into something countable, so "work on footwork" becomes ten minutes of lateral shuffles, three times this week.

High school: hand over the wheel. "Have you talked to the coach yet? What would you ask?" Your job shifts to helping your teen prepare that conversation, not having it for them.

## How to ask for the meeting

NFHS guidance for high school coaches, [published March 12, 2026](https://nfhs.org/stories/coaches-should-address-playing-time-issues-ahead-of-season), sets the ground rules: no playing-time talks on game days, dedicated meetings when a concern comes up, and a chain that runs athlete to coach, then coach to parent, then up the ladder if needed. Use those rules even if your program never said them out loud.

Send a short email, not a text and not a hallway ambush: "Hi Coach [Name], I'd like to set up a short meeting this week, whenever works for you, to understand [child's name]'s role on the team and what we can work on." A teenager should try that conversation first; for a younger child, the parent leads.

Bring your notes, your child's own view, and two or three questions. Leave the grievances home.

## Three questions that keep it useful

Ask what specific skills would earn more playing time. Then ask what a realistic timeline for getting there looks like, in weeks and not seasons. Last, ask whether anything at home or in extra practice would help.

Those three questions keep the whole conversation on development, the one part of the lineup a coach can actually answer for. Afterward, send a two-line recap: "Thanks for meeting. We talked about working on [skill] and checking back around [date]."

## When to go past the coach

Go to the athletic director or club director for a safety problem, such as an injured athlete being played against medical advice. Same for a broken written policy. Or for behavior that looks discriminatory, or a coach who refuses to meet at all.

Disagreement over who plays, and how much, is not on that list. An administrator will send you back to the coach, so arrive with dates, emails, and a short factual summary of what was agreed and what happened.

## What the coach is weighing

NFHS describes a range of program types. On a competitive team, playing time is earned by building the strongest lineup to win. A rebuilding program mixes results with experience, and freshman, intramural, or Unified Sports teams aim for equal participation.

Know which one your kid is on before you judge the minutes. Coaches also weigh fitness, technical skill, and tactical awareness, and they see Monday through Thursday while you see Saturday.

[USA Volleyball](https://usavolleyball.org/resource/the-coach-parent-dynamic/) passes along sport psychologist Frank Smoll's advice that coaches hold a pre-season or early-season parent meeting to explain their methods. ## Why the way you handle it matters

A [2022 study of adolescent soccer players](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36554464/) linked dropout to older age, less autonomy support from the coach, lower intrinsic motivation, being female, and lower socioeconomic status. Two of those are about motivation, and parents can shape motivation.

So protect your child's say in the goal, pair every criticism with one doable next step, and keep the talk about the team. A kid who watches a parent treat the bench as a problem to solve learns more than any lineup change can teach.

## FAQ

### Should parents complain to coaches about playing time?

Lead with a meeting request, not a complaint. NFHS guidance tells coaches to keep these talks off game days and to hear from the athlete first, then from the parent, and only then from anyone above the coach.

### What should I do when my child gets very little playing time?

Listen to your child, watch a practice and a game, and wait a day or two. If the pattern holds, email the coach to schedule a private meeting and bring specific questions about skills and timeline.

### Why do kids quit youth sports?

A 2022 study of adolescent soccer players tied dropout to lower autonomy support from coaches and weaker intrinsic motivation, along with age, sex, and socioeconomic status. Giving your child ownership of the next goal is one way to protect that motivation.

### What should I say when my kid doesn't get playing time?

Start with a question: "What do you think the coach is looking for right now?" Then help your child pick one skill to work on for two weeks.
