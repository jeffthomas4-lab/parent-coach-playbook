Sign it, read it, and show up for the preseason walkthrough. That's the whole job. A parent code of conduct works only when parents commit in writing and hear it explained out loud before the first game, not when they skim it once at registration and forget it exists.

Two things to do this week:

- Sign or acknowledge your league's code during registration, if your program has one.
- Attend or review the preseason meeting where the code gets walked through, since a code nobody explains rarely changes behavior.

[Little League](https://www.littleleague.org/university/articles/local-league-parents-code-of-conduct-help-make-parents-families-and-fans-accountable-for-their-behavior/) and [TrueSport](https://truesport.org/respect-accountability/dealing-disrespectful-parents-fans-stands/) both publish guidance you can borrow. ## What a parent code of conduct is and why it matters

A parent code of conduct is a written agreement about how parents behave at practices and games and around players, coaches, and officials. It covers sideline behavior, communication, and consequences.

Leagues didn't write these codes to police tone. A parent's sideline behavior shapes a kid's relationship with the sport, sometimes more than the coaching does, and kids notice whether your face after a mistake looks like disappointment or support.

TrueSport frames its guidance around respect and accountability. Little League treats its code as a way to make parents and fans accountable, not just a liability form.

## What should parents do and avoid at games?

Most codes boil down to a short list, and you can copy this one straight into a team handout or a preseason email to families.

**Do:**

- Cheer for effort, hustle, and bouncing back from mistakes, not just points.
- Treat every kid on both teams, and every official, with respect.
- Save game feedback for a calm conversation, not a shout from the stands.
- Model the behavior you want your kid to copy.

**Don't:**

- Yell instructions at your own kid during play.
- Approach or confront an official during or right after a game.
- Use profanity or raised voices near the field, court, or bench.
- Coach from the sideline if you aren't the coach, even quietly.
- Show up under the influence of alcohol or drugs.

The hardest moments are small. Your kid misses a serve and looks over at you, and what you do in those ten seconds matters more than almost anything else in the game.

**Pro Tip:** *Keep three phrases ready: "That one didn't go your way, and you'll get the next one," "I loved watching you try that," and "How are you feeling about it?" Our [sportsmanship guide](/game/13-14-sportsmanship/) has more for when you're rattled too.*

## How should leagues adopt and enforce a parent code?

A code in a binder nobody opens changes nothing, so build it into how the season actually runs from registration to the final game.

1. **Ask for a signed acknowledgement at registration.** New Jersey's [model athletic code of conduct](https://youthsports.rutgers.edu/articles/model-athletic-code-of-conduct/), written under P.L. 2002, Chapter 74, is a pledge in which the signer agrees to disciplinary action for violations. A signature at signup sets the tone before the season starts.
2. **Walk through it at a preseason meeting.** Hand it out on paper and digitally, then explain it out loud, which is what [makes it stick](https://thompsonlittleleague.org/Default.aspx?tabid=2115443).
3. **Enforce it in visible steps.** The [Niwot Youth Sports code](https://www.niwotyouthsports.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/NYS-Parent-Code-of-Conduct.pdf) lists a verbal warning, a written warning, a game suspension, a season suspension, and a lifetime suspension.

Whatever steps you choose, keep enforcement fair and predictable for every family:

- Record every incident with a date, what happened, and who saw it.
- Apply the same process to every parent, whoever their kid is.
- Hold disciplinary conversations in private.

## Sample parent pledge you can copy

No language yet? Adapt one of these.

**One-sentence version:** "I will support my child and this team with respect, patience, and encouragement, on and off the field."

**Longer version:** "I agree to respect coaches, officials, and players from both teams; to avoid coaching or criticizing from the sideline; to raise concerns calmly and privately, never during a game; and to understand that violating this code may result in warnings, suspension, or removal from the program."

Put the pledge on the registration form and again on the team page. A digital signoff works for most teams, and a paper copy signed at the preseason meeting adds a layer some families take more seriously.

## What if another parent breaks the code?

Stay calm. Your kid's experience matters far more than being right in the moment, even when the other parent is clearly out of line.

1. **In the moment:** don't confront the other parent. Focus on your kid, and step away if voices rise nearby.
2. **That night:** write down the date, time, exact words or actions, and who else saw it.
3. **Wait 24 hours** before raising it with the coach, so a heated moment doesn't turn into a bigger one.
4. **Escalate to league officials if it happens again.** A written pattern gives them something to act on.
5. **Call law enforcement right away** if there's any threat of violence or physical contact.

**Pro Tip:** *One ugly moment usually ends with a quiet word. A pattern needs a paper trail, so write down even the small incidents that seem too minor to mention at the time.*

## Where can I find model codes to adapt?

Start with Little League's guidance on parent codes and TrueSport's respect-and-accountability material. New Jersey's model code from Rutgers shows signed acknowledgement with listed consequences, and if games happen on school property, check your district's policy first.

## Why clear parent codes protect the whole team

The codes that work aren't the strictest ones. They're the ones parents hear explained, not just sign.

A preseason walkthrough turns a form into a shared understanding, and that changes how a whole team's parents act by October. We built our sportsmanship scripts around the same idea: give parents the words before they need them.
