The best tee ball drills are the ones that get every kid swinging, throwing, and running before boredom sets in: tee hitting on three or four stations at once, partner toss, bucket-target throwing, rolling ground balls, and a base-running game to close things out. Run them station-based in short blocks, keep the mood fun-first over technical, and keep practice short: 30 to 45 minutes for 3 and 4-year-olds, 45 to 60 for 5 to 7-year-olds. That single framework solves most of what makes tee ball practice hard to plan.

## Eight Quick Tee Ball Drills You Can Run Today

You don't need a coaching manual. These eight drills cover hitting, throwing, catching, and running, and most of them take under two minutes to set up before the kids arrive.

- **Tee-hitting rotation.** Set up 3 to 4 tees with a parent helper at each one. Ages 3 to 7. Cue: "Squish the bug, swing level." Keeps lines short so nobody waits more than a minute or two.
- **Alligator catch.** Kids kneel and "chomp" a rolled ball between two hands like jaws closing. Ages 4 to 6. Cue: "Big mouth, soft hands." It shows up in municipal [t-ball drill guides](https://www.rogersar.gov/DocumentCenter/View/9556/T-Ball-Drills-PDF) as a fear-free way to teach ground balls.
- **Triangle grounders.** Three kids form a triangle and roll a ball around it, fielding and resetting. Ages 5 to 7. Cue: "Glove down, eyes on the ball."
- **Bucket-target throw.** Line up buckets at 10 to 15 feet and have kids throw beanbags or soft balls into them. Ages 4 to 7. Cue: "Step, then throw."
- **Partner toss streaks.** Two kids toss underhand and count how many catches in a row. Ages 5 to 7. Cue: "Soft hands, watch it in."
- **Pop-up countdown.** Coach tosses a soft ball straight up; kids count down and catch before it lands. Ages 5 to 7. Cue: "Track it all the way."
- **Beach-ball batting.** Swap the wiffle ball for a beach ball on the tee for the youngest hitters. Ages 3 to 4. Cue: "Big swing, no fear."
- **Jog-the-bases loop.** Everyone jogs the bases together in a line, touching each bag. Ages 3 to 7. Cue: "Touch every base."

Keep lines moving by running multiple stations at once and assigning one parent helper per station. A single tee with 12 kids waiting is where tee ball practice goes sideways fast.

## How Long Should a Tee Ball Practice Actually Run?

The [Little League Tee Ball Parents Guide](https://www.littleleague.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Tee-Ball-Parents-Guide.pdf) recommends two activities a week, each lasting 45 minutes to 1 hour and 15 minutes. We'd stay near the low end: 45 to 60 minutes for ages 5 to 7, and 30 to 45 for ages 3 to 4.

At this age, attention spans are measured in minutes, not innings. A 4-year-old can focus for about one good station rotation, then needs to move, which is why short blocks beat one long lecture-style drill.

Ten minutes in line loses a 5-year-old faster than a bad hop ever could.

**Our rule of thumb:** split practice 50/50 between skill instruction and active play, so young kids spend more time moving than standing and listening.

A few timing anchors to build a practice around:

- Station blocks: 6 to 10 minutes each, then rotate.
- Adult-to-child ratio: aim for one helper per station of 4 to 6 kids.
- Total skill time vs. play time: split it close to evenly.
- Whole practice: we cap it at 60 minutes, even for the oldest tee ball group.

## Running Hitting Stations Without the Long Line

Hitting is where most tee ball practices bottleneck, and it's almost always because there's one tee and fifteen kids. The fix is simple: run 3 to 4 tees at once, each staffed by a parent helper, and rotate small groups through every 6 to 8 minutes.

1. **Set up 3 to 4 tees** spaced at least 10 feet apart with a bucket of wiffle or soft balls at each one. Local [tee ball guidelines](https://owllba.ca/Public/Documents/owll_t-ball_guidelines_2026.pdf) and Little League's curriculum both build their drills around soft balls.
2. **Assign one parent per tee** to reset the ball, adjust tee height, and give one cue per swing. One cue only, because a kid trying to fix grip, stance, and follow-through at once fixes none of them.
3. **Run "Hit the Target."** Place colored cones at different spots in the field and call out a color before each swing. This turns straight repetition into a small game and teaches kids to notice where the ball actually goes.
4. **Swap in a beach ball for the youngest hitters.** For 3 and 4-year-olds still building coordination, a beach ball on the tee removes the intimidation and lets them focus on swinging.
5. **Target 5 to 6 swings per kid per rotation**, then move the group to the next station. More than that loses the younger ones.

**Pro Tip:** *Have the "on deck" kid stand well behind the hitter, not beside the tee. A swinging bat is the closest hazard at a tee station, so spacing around the tee matters as much as spacing in the field.*

A [station-based approach with an adult helper at each tee](https://baseballpositive.com/plans-tee-ball/) is the biggest lever for cutting wait time, even with twelve 5-year-olds on the field.

## Throwing Drills That Build Accuracy Step by Step

Start every throwing progression soft and close. Beanbags or foam balls at 5 to 8 feet let kids focus on mechanics without worrying about catching something that stings.

Once that feels comfortable, move to a real (but soft) ball and stretch the distance to 10 to 15 feet.

From there, two drills carry most of the load:

- **Partner toss.** Pair kids up and have them count consecutive catches, resetting the streak if the ball hits the ground. This builds both accuracy and patience.
- **Bucket targets.** Line up buckets or hoops at varying distances and let kids aim for them one at a time. A hoop hung on a fence works just as well if buckets aren't available.

The Little League Tee Ball curriculum teaches the arm path with a simple reminder: the thumb goes past the thigh as the ball comes out of the glove, then the ball keeps going up. It's a cue kids can feel, not an abstract note about arm angle.

Pair it with "step to target" before the throw. That covers the two big breakdowns at this age, arm-only throwing and heaving the ball instead of aiming it.

If throwing mechanics feel shaky across your whole group, [Parent Coach Desk's youth throwing drills guide](/game/baseball-throwing-drill/) breaks the progression down further with drills built specifically for developing arms.

Keep groups small during target work, no more than 3 or 4 kids per bucket or hoop, so nobody stands around waiting for a turn longer than 30 seconds.

## Catching and Fielding Drills Kids Actually Enjoy

Catching at this age is mostly about removing fear, not perfecting form. A rolled ball feels less threatening than a thrown one, so every fielding progression should start on the ground and work up.

1. **Roll it first.** Coach or helper rolls a soft ball directly at each kid, one at a time, keeping the line moving fast.
2. **Underhand toss next.** Once ground balls feel easy, switch to a soft underhand toss at chest height.
3. **Pop-up countdown last.** Toss the ball straight up and have the group count down together before the catch. Kids get comfortable with this last, so save it for later.
4. **Alligator catch for the youngest.** Kneeling and "chomping" a rolled ball with two hands works for 3 and 4-year-olds who aren't ready to track a bouncing ball standing up.
5. **Triangle grounders to finish.** Three kids in a triangle roll and field continuously, which keeps everyone moving instead of standing in a fielding line.

**Pro Tip:** *Teach one fielding cue and repeat it every single time: "glove down, bare hand on top, scoop to the belly." Rhythm and repetition beat a string of separate instructions.*

Rotate through these fast. A fielding line where kids wait 90 seconds between turns is where you'll start losing attention, so keep groups at 3 or 4 kids per line at most.

## Base Running Games That Teach Without Feeling Like Drills

Base running is the easiest skill to disguise as play, which makes it the perfect way to end a practice on a high note.

- **Jog-the-bases.** The whole group jogs the base path together, touching every bag, at the start or end of practice.
- **Sprint-to-first sets.** Line kids up at home and have them sprint through first base rather than stopping on it, which mirrors real game mechanics.
- **Coach-calls base drill.** Shout a base name and have kids run to it, mixing in listening skills with base recognition.
- **Relay races.** Split into two lines and race base to base, which turns pure conditioning into something kids beg to do again.

Use base running as both a warm-up and a closer. It needs little explanation, burns off early energy, and ends practice on a game instead of a lecture.

## A Ready-to-Run 45-Minute Tee Ball Practice

Copy this practice for this week. It follows a common template pattern of [putting the hardest skill work in the middle](https://joinstriveon.com/blog/tee-ball-practice-plan), with an active warm-up before it and a game after.

1. **Warm-up game (5 minutes):** Jog-the-bases or a simple tag game to burn early energy.
2. **Throwing station (8 minutes):** Beanbag toss progressing to partner toss.
3. **Hitting station (12 minutes):** 3 to 4 tees running simultaneously, 5 to 6 swings per kid.
4. **Fielding station (8 minutes):** Roll to underhand toss progression, alligator catch for the youngest group.
5. **Base running (7 minutes):** Sprint-to-first sets and a coach-calls drill.
6. **Mini scrimmage (5 minutes):** Live at-bats with base running to connect everything to real play.

| Time Block | Activity | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 minutes | Warm-up game | 5 min |
| 5 to 13 minutes | Throwing station | 8 min |
| 13 to 25 minutes | Hitting stations | 12 min |
| 25 to 33 minutes | Fielding station | 8 min |
| 33 to 40 minutes | Base running | 7 min |
| 40 to 45 minutes | Mini scrimmage | 5 min |

For a 60-minute version, add a dedicated fielding-only station (ground balls plus pop-ups) and stretch the closing scrimmage to 10 minutes so more kids get a full at-bat. Assign parent helpers before the first whistle: one per tee, one on the throwing line, and one on base running. Quick transitions depend on helpers already knowing their job.

## Building a Season: A Simple Week-by-Week Progression

Skills build best when they layer instead of pile up all at once. Little League's 10-week Tee Ball curriculum gives volunteer coaches weekly practice and game plans with drill diagrams, so nobody plans from scratch.

A simple 10-week cadence:

- **Weeks 1 to 2:** Throwing and catching fundamentals, lots of beanbag and rolling work.
- **Weeks 3 to 4:** Introduce tee hitting stations and basic fielding.
- **Weeks 5 to 6:** Add base running games and combine hitting with fielding stations.
- **Weeks 7 to 8:** Introduce soft toss for kids ready for it, keep tees running for everyone else.
- **Weeks 9 to 10:** Full mini scrimmages that combine every skill learned so far.

Introduce soft toss only once most of the group is consistently making contact off the tee, often around week 6 or 7. Never force it on kids who aren't ready.

**Takeaways Table**

| Season Phase | Focus | Watch For |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–2 | Throwing, catching basics | Kids avoiding the ball out of fear |
| Weeks 3–4 | Tee hitting, basic fielding | Long wait lines at single tees |
| Weeks 5–6 | Base running, combined stations | Confusion on which base to run to |
| Weeks 7–8 | Optional soft toss | Frustration from kids not yet ready |
| Weeks 9–10 | Scrimmage integration | Overexcitement, kids forgetting rules |

Tape a weekly equipment checklist inside your gear bag to skip the "who has the cones" scramble.

## What to Pack and How to Keep Practice Safe

A tee ball practice runs on a short list of gear, most of which fits in one bag. Pack: [helmets](/go/baseball-helmet-youth/) for every batter, 3 to 4 [tees](/go/baseball-batting-tee-youth-grow-with-me/), extra [bats](/go/baseball-bat-teeball/) sized for small hands, [wiffle or soft balls](/go/baseball-balls-wiffle/), [cones](/go/agility-cones-set/) for target drills, [buckets](/go/baseball-ball-bucket-with-lid/) for ball storage and target practice, and enough water for the whole group.

Safety at this age comes down to spacing. Keep the "on deck" kid well behind the batter, never beside the tee, and set tee stations at least 10 feet apart so a wild swing at one doesn't reach the next.

No running with bats, ever. Repeat that rule loudly at every practice.

- Helmets on before any bat comes out, no exceptions.
- One adult helper stationed at each tee or drill station.
- Clear rule: bats get set down, never carried while running.
- Water breaks built into transitions, not treated as a separate stop.

Assign parent helpers specific roles before practice starts: a tee manager, a bucket manager, a helmet checker, and a base-running coordinator. Splitting jobs ahead of time speeds up every single transition between stations, and it frees the head coach to watch the kids.

**Pro Tip:** *Print a one-page role sheet and hand it to helpers as they arrive. It works.*

## Practical Coaching Scripts and Where to Learn More

Coaching tee ball is less about baseball knowledge and more about managing a group of 5-year-olds who all want a turn right now. A few short scripts help more than any technical explanation:

- **After an error:** "Nice try, shake it off, next play's yours." Keep it short, keep moving.
- **Redirecting a distracted kid:** "Hey, I need your eyes over here for one second." Then give one instruction, not three.
- **Enlisting a helper:** "Can you be my bucket boss today?" Kids at this age love having a named job.

Keep every cue simple and every tone upbeat, because fun-first coaching is what makes the skills stick. ## Warming Up and Cooling Down Without Losing the Group

A tee ball warm-up doesn't need to look like a real practice. Five minutes of jogging the bases, a light game of freeze tag, or arm circles disguised as "airplane arms" gets blood moving and joints loose without feeling like a chore to a 5-year-old.

Keep the warm-up moving. Standing in a circle stretching quads means nothing to a kid this age and burns attention span before the real work starts, while jogging bases or a chase game does the same job.

Cool-downs matter more than most volunteer coaches realize, mostly as a mental transition out of practice. A short walk-through of the bases at a slow pace, followed by a quick group huddle recapping one thing that went well, gives kids a clear signal that practice is ending and helps prevent the mad scramble of parents pulling kids off the field mid-drill.

Skip adult-style stretching routines, which mostly just lose the room. What helps more is keeping intensity moderate instead of sprinting kids hard right before pickup, and building water breaks into transitions before anyone overheats.

If a kid seems tired, overheated, or reluctant to keep moving during the cool-down, check in with them instead of pushing through the plan. Tee ball practice should never be the reason a 5-year-old associates sports with exhaustion.

## The Mistakes That Sink Most Tee Ball Practices

The single biggest mistake is running one tee for the whole group. It guarantees long lines, bored kids, and a coach spending more time managing a queue than teaching anything. The fix is always the same: more tees, more helpers, shorter rotations.

A close second is stacking too many cues onto one swing or throw. Telling a 5-year-old to fix their grip, their stance, their eyes, and their follow-through in the same rep overloads a brain that's still learning to coordinate basic movement. Pick one cue per drill and repeat it all season.

Long explanations are another common trap. A two-minute talk on batting stance loses a 4-year-old by the second sentence. Demonstrate the swing once, give the one-word cue, then let kids swing.

Some coaches also skip the fun elements entirely, treating tee ball like a scaled-down version of real baseball instruction. That backfires. Kids this age remember whether practice was fun far more than whether their swing mechanics were technically sound, and the fun is what keeps them coming back next season.

Finally, watch for uneven reps. It's easy for a shy or slower kid to end up at the back of every line and get half the swings or throws of a more assertive teammate. Rotate deliberately, and count reps out loud if you have to, so every kid gets an equal share.

## Why Fun-First Coaching Beats Technical Perfection Here

A lot of tee ball advice treats it like junior varsity baseball: fix the stance, correct the grip, drill fundamentals until they're clean. That advice aims at the wrong priority. At 5 years old, the swing mechanics matter far less than whether the kid wants to come back next Saturday.

Keep it simple: short blocks, constant movement, and as much play as skill work. Little League's own curriculum is built on fun-focused stations, and in our experience long technical sessions shrink a roster by midseason.

New coach? Prioritize this: get more tees than you think you need, assign helpers before kids arrive, and pick one cue per skill and stick with it. The finer points of swing mechanics can wait.

A kid still excited in week 8 will improve, and a kid who quit from boredom in week 3 never gets the chance.
