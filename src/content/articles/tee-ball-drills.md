The best tee ball drills are the ones that get every kid swinging, throwing, and running before boredom sets in: tee hitting on three or four stations at once, partner toss, bucket-target throwing, rolling ground balls, and a base-running game to close things out. Run them station-based in short blocks, keep the mood fun-first over technical, and cap practice at a maximum time recommended for these age groups, shorter for 3 and 4-year-olds and longer for 5 to 7-year-olds. That single framework solves most of what makes tee ball practice hard to plan.

***

> **TL;DR:**
>
> - Running multiple hitting stations simultaneously with parent helpers prevents long lines and keeps kids engaged during practice.
> - Practice sessions should last no more than 45 to 60 minutes, with short station blocks of 6 to 10 minutes to suit young attention spans.
> - Prioritizing fun-based drills and continuous movement leads to more repeat participation than focusing solely on technical skill perfection.
> - Using simple cues and limiting instructions helps young kids understand and perform skills without feeling overwhelmed.
> - Consistent rotation, clear rules, and deliberate group sizing ensure safety, fairness, and effective skill development throughout practices.

## Eight Quick Tee Ball Drills You Can Run Today

You don't need a coaching manual to get a solid practice going this week. These eight drills cover hitting, throwing, catching, and running, and most take under two minutes to set up.

- **Tee-hitting rotation.** Set up 3 to 4 tees with a parent helper at each one. Ages 3 to 7. Cue: "Squish the bug, swing level." Keeps lines short so nobody waits more than a minute or two.
- **Alligator catch.** Kids kneel and "chomp" a rolled ball between two hands like jaws closing. Ages 4 to 6. Cue: "Big mouth, soft hands." This one comes straight from municipal [t-ball drill guides](https://www.rogersar.gov/DocumentCenter/View/9556/T-Ball-Drills-PDF) that use it to teach ground-ball fundamentals without fear.
- **Triangle grounders.** Three kids form a triangle and roll a ball around it, fielding and resetting. Ages 5 to 7. Cue: "Glove down, eyes on the ball."
- **Bucket-target throw.** Line up buckets at 10 to 15 feet and have kids throw beanbags or soft balls into them. Ages 4 to 7. Cue: "Step, then throw."
- **Partner toss streaks.** Two kids toss underhand and count how many catches in a row. Ages 5 to 7. Cue: "Soft hands, watch it in."
- **Pop-up countdown.** Coach tosses a soft ball straight up; kids count down and catch before it lands. Ages 5 to 7. Cue: "Track it all the way."
- **Beach-ball batting.** Swap the wiffle ball for a beach ball on the tee for the youngest hitters. Ages 3 to 4. Cue: "Big swing, no fear."
- **Jog-the-bases loop.** Everyone jogs the bases together in a line, touching each bag. Ages 3 to 7. Cue: "Touch every base."

Keep lines moving by running multiple stations at once and assigning one parent helper per station. A single tee with 12 kids waiting is where tee ball practice goes sideways fast.

## How Long Should a Tee Ball Practice Actually Run?

Little League's own guidance sets the outer limit: 45 to 60 minutes for ages 5 to 7, and 30 to 45 minutes for ages 3 to 4, with a roughly even split between skill work and active play, per the [Little League Tee Ball Parents Guide](https://www.littleleague.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/Tee-Ball-Parents-Guide.pdf). That's not a suggestion to stretch toward. It's closer to a ceiling.

At this age, attention spans are measured in minutes, not innings. A 4-year-old can focus hard for about the length of one good station rotation, then needs to move. That's why shorter blocks with built-in movement beat one long lecture-style drill every time. Standing in a line listening to instructions for ten minutes will lose a 5-year-old faster than a bad hop ever could.

**Statistic Callout:** Little League recommends splitting practice roughly 50/50 between skill instruction and active play, a ratio that keeps young kids engaged and moving rather than standing and listening.

A few timing anchors to build a practice around:

- Station blocks: 6 to 10 minutes each, then rotate.
- Adult-to-child ratio: aim for one helper per station of 4 to 6 kids.
- Total skill time vs. play time: split it close to evenly, per Little League's own guidance.
- Whole practice: never longer than 60 minutes, even for the oldest tee ball group.

## Running Hitting Stations Without the Long Line

Hitting is where most tee ball practices bottleneck, and it's almost always because there's one tee and fifteen kids. The fix is simple: run 3 to 4 tees at once, each staffed by a parent helper, and rotate small groups through every 6 to 8 minutes.

1. **Set up 3 to 4 tees** spaced at least 10 feet apart with a bucket of wiffle or soft balls at each one. Community coaching guides consistently recommend soft balls over hard ones at this stage, both for safety and to reduce the fear of getting hit, according to [youth tee ball guidelines](https://owllba.ca/Public/Documents/owll_t-ball_guidelines_2026.pdf).
2. **Assign one parent per tee** to reset the ball, adjust tee height, and give one cue per swing. One cue only. A kid trying to fix their grip, their stance, and their follow-through at once usually fixes none of them.
3. **Run "Hit the Target."** Place colored cones at different spots in the field and call out a color before each swing. This turns straight repetition into a small game and teaches kids to notice where the ball actually goes.
4. **Swap in a beach ball for the youngest hitters.** For 3 and 4-year-olds still building coordination, a beach ball on the tee removes the intimidation factor entirely and lets them focus on the swing mechanics.
5. **Target 5 to 6 swings per kid per rotation**, then move the group to the next station. More than that and you'll lose the younger ones to fidgeting.

**Pro Tip:** *Have the "on deck" kid stand well behind the hitter, not beside the tee. Most tee ball injuries at practice happen from bat swings, not batted balls, so spacing around the tee matters as much as spacing in the field.*

A [station-based approach with an adult helper at each tee](https://baseballpositive.com/plans-tee-ball/) is the single biggest lever for cutting wait time, and it's the difference between a practice that feels chaotic and one that feels organized even with twelve 5-year-olds on the field.

## Throwing Drills That Build Accuracy Step by Step

Start every throwing progression with something soft and close. Beanbags or foam balls at 5 to 8 feet let kids focus purely on mechanics without worrying about catching something that stings. Once that feels comfortable, move to a real (but soft) ball and stretch the distance to 10 to 15 feet.

From there, two drills carry most of the load:

- **Partner toss.** Pair kids up and have them count consecutive catches, resetting the streak if the ball hits the ground. This builds both accuracy and patience.
- **Bucket targets.** Line up buckets or hoops at varying distances and let kids aim for them one at a time. A hoop hung on a fence works just as well if buckets aren't available.

The Little League Tee Ball curriculum teaches throwing follow-through with a simple phrase: "thumb to thigh, show it to the sky." It's a physical cue kids can actually feel, not an abstract instruction about arm angle. Pair that with "step to target" before the throw, and you've covered the two biggest mechanical breakdowns at this age: throwing with the whole body instead of just the arm, and aiming instead of just heaving the ball forward.

If throwing mechanics feel shaky across your whole group, [Parent Coach Desk's youth throwing drills guide](/game/baseball-throwing-drill/) breaks the progression down further with drills built specifically for developing arms.

Keep groups small during target work, no more than 3 or 4 kids per bucket or hoop, so nobody stands around waiting for a turn longer than 30 seconds.

## Catching and Fielding Drills Kids Actually Enjoy

Catching at this age is mostly about removing fear, not perfecting form. A rolled ball feels less threatening than a thrown one, so every fielding progression should start on the ground and work up.

1. **Roll it first.** Coach or helper rolls a soft ball directly at each kid, one at a time, keeping the line moving fast.
2. **Underhand toss next.** Once ground balls feel easy, switch to a soft underhand toss at chest height.
3. **Pop-up countdown last.** Toss the ball straight up and have the group count down together before the catch. This is usually the last skill kids feel confident with, so save it for once the group is warmed up.
4. **Alligator catch for the youngest.** Kneeling and "chomping" a rolled ball with two hands works well for 3 and 4-year-olds who aren't ready to track a bouncing ball standing up, a drill borrowed from municipal tee ball drill sheets.
5. **Triangle grounders to finish.** Three kids in a triangle roll and field continuously, which keeps everyone moving instead of standing in a fielding line.

**Pro Tip:** *Teach one fielding cue and repeat it every single time: "glove down, bare hand on top, scoop to the belly." Kids at this age remember rhythm and repetition far better than a string of separate instructions.*

Rotate through these fast. A fielding line where kids wait 90 seconds between turns is where you'll start losing attention, so keep groups at 3 or 4 kids per line at most.

## Base Running Games That Teach Without Feeling Like Drills

Base running is the easiest skill to disguise as play, which makes it the perfect way to end a practice on a high note.

- **Jog-the-bases.** The whole group jogs the base path together, touching every bag, at the start or end of practice.
- **Sprint-to-first sets.** Line kids up at home and have them sprint through first base rather than stopping on it, which mirrors real game mechanics.
- **Coach-calls base drill.** Shout a base name and have kids run to it, mixing in listening skills with base recognition.
- **Relay races.** Split into two lines and race base to base, which turns pure conditioning into something kids beg to do again.

Use base running as both a warm-up and a closer. It doesn't require much explanation, it burns off early-practice energy, and it's a natural way to end on a game instead of a lecture.

## A Ready-to-Run 45-Minute Tee Ball Practice

Here's a full practice you can copy for this week, built around short blocks and constant movement. Practice templates consistently recommend [placing the hardest skill work in the middle](https://joinstriveon.com/blog/tee-ball-practice-plan), bookended by an active warm-up and a game, which is exactly how this one is structured.

1. **Warm-up game (5 minutes):** Jog-the-bases or a simple tag game to burn early energy.
2. **Throwing station (8 minutes):** Beanbag toss progressing to partner toss.
3. **Hitting station (12 minutes):** 3 to 4 tees running simultaneously, 5 to 6 swings per kid.
4. **Fielding station (8 minutes):** Roll to underhand toss progression, alligator catch for the youngest group.
5. **Base running (7 minutes):** Sprint-to-first sets and a coach-calls drill.
6. **Mini scrimmage (5 minutes):** Live at-bats with base running to connect everything to real play.

| Time Block | Activity | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 minutes | Warm-up game | 5 min |
| 5 to 8 minutes | Throwing station | 8 min |
| 8 to 12 minutes | Hitting stations | 12 min |
| 12 to 15 minutes | Fielding station | 8 min |

| 15 to 20 minutes | Base running | 7 min |
| 20 to 25 minutes | Mini scrimmage | 5 min |

For a 60-minute version, add a dedicated fielding-only station (ground balls plus pop-ups) and stretch the closing scrimmage to 10 minutes so more kids get a full at-bat. Assign parent helpers before the first whistle blows: one per hitting tee, one running the throwing line, and one on base running. Quick transitions depend entirely on helpers already knowing their job, not on the head coach shouting instructions between every block.

## Building a Season: A Simple Week-by-Week Progression

Skills build best when they layer instead of pile up all at once. Little League's own 10-week Tee Ball curriculum gives volunteer coaches weekly templates and activity diagrams so nobody has to plan from scratch, and it's worth pulling up if you want a full season mapped out already.

A simple 10-week cadence looks something like this:

- **Weeks 1 to 2:** Throwing and catching fundamentals, lots of beanbag and rolling work.
- **Weeks 3 to 4:** Introduce tee hitting stations and basic fielding.
- **Weeks 5 to 6:** Add base running games and combine hitting with fielding stations.
- **Weeks 7 to 8:** Introduce soft toss for kids ready for it, keep tees running for everyone else.
- **Weeks 9 to 10:** Full mini scrimmages that combine every skill learned so far.

Introduce soft toss only once most of the group is consistently making contact off the tee, usually somewhere around week 6 or 7, and never force it on kids who aren't ready. It's a bonus rep, not a requirement.

**Takeaways Table**

| Season Phase | Focus | Watch For |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–2 | Throwing, catching basics | Kids avoiding the ball out of fear |
| Weeks 3–4 | Tee hitting, basic fielding | Long wait lines at single tees |
| Weeks 5–6 | Base running, combined stations | Confusion on which base to run to |
| Weeks 7–8 | Optional soft toss | Frustration from kids not yet ready |
| Weeks 9–10 | Scrimmage integration | Overexcitement, kids forgetting rules |

Keep a simple equipment checklist for each week taped inside your gear bag. It saves the "wait, who has the cones" scramble every single Saturday.

## What to Pack and How to Keep Practice Safe

A tee ball practice runs on a short list of gear, most of which fits in one bag. Pack: helmets for every batter, 3 to 4 tees, extra bats sized for small hands, wiffle or soft balls, cones for target drills, buckets for ball storage and target practice, and enough water for the whole group.

Safety at this age comes down to spacing more than anything else. Keep the "on deck" kid well behind the batter, never beside the tee. No running with bats, ever, and make that rule loud and repeated every single practice. Give each tee station enough room, at least 10 feet apart, so a wild swing at one station doesn't reach the next.

- Helmets on before any bat comes out, no exceptions.
- One adult helper stationed at each tee or drill station.
- Clear rule: bats get set down, never carried while running.
- Water breaks built into transitions, not treated as a separate stop.

Assign parent helpers specific roles before practice starts: a tee manager, a bucket manager, a helmet checker, and a base-running coordinator. Splitting these jobs ahead of time speeds up every transition during practice.

**Pro Tip:** *Print a one-page role sheet and hand it to helpers as they arrive. Even a rough checklist beats explaining each job fresh every single week.*

## Practical Coaching Scripts and Where to Learn More

Coaching tee ball is less about baseball knowledge and more about managing a group of 5-year-olds who all want a turn right now. A few short scripts help more than any technical explanation:

- **After an error:** "Nice try, shake it off, next play's yours." Keep it short, keep moving.
- **Redirecting a distracted kid:** "Hey, I need your eyes over here for one second." Then give one instruction, not three.
- **Enlisting a helper:** "Can you be my bucket boss today?" Kids at this age love having a named job.

Keep every cue simple and every tone upbeat. Fun-first coaching isn't a compromise on teaching real skills, it's what makes the skills stick. ## Warming Up and Cooling Down Without Losing the Group

A tee ball warm-up doesn't need to look like a real practice. Five minutes of jogging the bases, a light game of freeze tag, or arm circles disguised as "airplane arms" gets blood moving and joints loose without feeling like a chore to a 5-year-old.

Keep the warm-up active rather than static. Standing in a circle stretching quads means nothing to a kid this age and burns valuable attention span before the real work even starts. A moving warm-up, like jogging bases or a quick chase game, accomplishes the same physical goal while keeping engagement high.

Cool-downs matter more than most volunteer coaches realize, mostly for the mental transition out of practice rather than muscle recovery. A short walk-through of the bases at a slow pace, followed by a quick group huddle recapping one thing that went well, gives kids a clear signal that practice is ending and helps prevent the mad scramble of parents pulling kids off the field mid-drill.

Skip anything resembling adult-style stretching routines. At this age, there's little evidence it prevents injury, and it mostly just loses the room. What actually helps: keeping activity intensity moderate throughout practice rather than sprinting kids hard right before pickup, and making sure water breaks happen during natural transitions rather than as an afterthought once someone's already overheated.

If a kid seems tired, overheated, or reluctant to keep moving during the cool-down, that's worth a quick check-in rather than pushing through to finish the plan. Tee ball practice should never be the reason a 5-year-old associates sports with exhaustion.

## The Mistakes That Sink Most Tee Ball Practices

The single biggest mistake is running one tee for the whole group. It guarantees long lines, bored kids, and a coach spending more time managing a queue than teaching anything. The fix is always the same: more tees, more helpers, shorter rotations.

A close second is stacking too many cues onto one swing or throw. Telling a 5-year-old to fix their grip, their stance, their eyes, and their follow-through in the same rep overloads a brain that's still learning to coordinate basic movement. Pick one cue per drill and repeat it consistently across the whole season instead of introducing a new correction every week.

Long explanations are another common trap. A two-minute verbal breakdown of proper batting stance loses a 4-year-old before you finish the second sentence. Show, don't lecture: demonstrate the swing once, give the one-word cue, then let kids swing.

Some coaches also skip the fun elements entirely, treating tee ball like a scaled-down version of real baseball instruction. That backfires. Kids this age remember whether practice was fun far more than whether their swing mechanics were technically sound, and the fun is what keeps them coming back next season.

Finally, watch for uneven reps. It's easy for a shy or slower kid to end up at the back of every line and get half the swings or throws of a more assertive teammate. Rotate deliberately, and count reps out loud if you have to, so every kid gets a genuinely equal share of the practice.

## Why Fun-First Coaching Beats Technical Perfection Here

The conventional advice on tee ball tends to treat it like junior varsity baseball: fix the stance, correct the grip, drill the fundamentals until they're clean. That advice isn't wrong exactly, it's just aimed at the wrong priority. At 5 years old, the swing mechanics matter far less than whether the kid wants to come back next Saturday.

What the research on practice structure actually supports is simpler than most coaching clinics make it sound: short blocks, constant movement, and a near-even split between skill work and play. That's not a compromise for younger kids, it's the actual formula that works, straight from Little League's own guidance. Coaches who ignore it in favor of longer, more technical sessions usually end up with a smaller, more frustrated roster by midseason.

If you're a first-time volunteer coach staring down your first practice, prioritize this: get more tees than you think you need, assign helpers before kids arrive, and pick one cue per skill and stick with it. Everything else, the finer points of swing mechanics or fielding form, can wait. A kid who's still excited to show up in week 8 will improve. A kid who quit in week 3 out of boredom never gets the chance.
