Your kid walks into a gym with 40 kids they've never met. The coach is picking 12. Your kid can do that math.

For a lot of 11- and 12-year-olds, this is the first tryout where the cut is real and everyone can see it coming.

## Before: lower the stakes without lying

Give them the honest version the night before:

*A lot of good kids are trying out. You might make it and you might not, but either way you're playing somewhere this season, and that part is already settled.*

Skip the promises. *You're definitely making it* and *the coach will see how good you are* both sound kind, but neither one is yours to promise, and your kid will remember that you said it.

Tell them what's being measured, too. Skill counts, and so do effort against a tougher group, staying composed when a drill goes badly, and taking a correction without sulking.

The [tryout drill guide](/drive-there/how-to-read-the-tryout-drill/) walks through what each kind of drill is built to show.

## After: ask about the game, not the result

Use the same question no matter how it went. *What did you notice about how the other kids played, and what was different from your team?*

That question turns the drive home into scouting. It also builds a habit worth keeping for years: pulling something useful out of a hard day.

## Either result teaches something

If they don't make it, they learn that hard work doesn't guarantee a spot. That stings. It's also how a lot of kids figure out what to work on next.

Start with the [24-hour rule](/drive-home/after-the-cut-the-24-hour-rule/) before anyone makes a big decision. The email to the coach, the switch to another club, and the private-lesson plan can all wait until everyone has slept on it.

If they make it, they learn that the next level expects more. Expect the first few practices to feel faster than anything they've done so far, and that's normal.
