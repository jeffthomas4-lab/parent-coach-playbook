My co-coach told me over text in June that he wasn't coming back. Two lines. His son had aged out of the age group, and there was no reason left for him to give up four nights a week to a team that wasn't his anymore.

I read it twice, standing in my kitchen. Then I felt something I didn't expect. Grief, for a friendship I hadn't noticed was built entirely inside a job that had just ended.

## The friendship only existed because of the job

We didn't become friends the normal way. We became friends because practice started at six and someone had to run cones while the other set up goals.

Three seasons of that builds something real. You learn how someone argues with a ref. You learn what they do in the fourth quarter when the team is down eight and scared.

None of that transfers automatically once the practice schedule disappears. The friendship was real. The container that held it together wasn't permanent, and most of us never notice the container until it's already gone.

## Nobody sends a card for this

When a marriage ends, people know what to do. When a friend moves away, there's a going-away dinner. When a coaching partnership ends, there's no ritual at all, just a group text that goes quiet in August when the season starts without one of you.

The kids don't grieve it either. They liked the guy fine, but he wasn't their coach the way the parent running the whole show was. The other parents barely notice, and from the sideline it looks like nothing more than a roster change.

That's what makes it heavier instead of lighter: a real loss that has no name, no ritual, and no line on anyone's calendar marking that it happened, and it keeps costing something long after the season moves on without him. Quietly. Longer than you'd guess.

## What actually happens if you let it slide

Most of these friendships don't end on purpose. They end from pure attrition, one skipped beer at a time. You mean to grab that beer in the off-season, and you don't, because nothing forces the meeting anymore.

By next spring, if the group text still exists, it's just logistics again. Not because either of you stopped caring. Because nobody ever replaces the machinery that used to force you into the same parking lot four nights a week, three seasons running, until the day it just stopped.

## It isn't just coaches

The same thing happens to the parent who ran the sign-up sheet with you for three years, or the two of you who split scorekeeping every Saturday because neither one wanted the whole slate. To the carpool partner whose kid switches schools. Tuesday afternoons, gone with them.

Any partnership built to solve a season's logistics can turn into a real friendship without either person clocking exactly when it happened, and any of them can end the exact same way this one did. Quietly. With no card, in a text that shows up in June.

## Say it before it goes quiet

Call him before next season starts this time, not after the whole thing has already gone quiet on its own. Ask him to something that has nothing to do with the team.

A game on TV. A kid's birthday party you'd go to anyway. Anything that has nothing to do with a whistle, a roster, or a Tuesday practice schedule.

Say the actual thing, too. "I'm going to miss coaching with you" is a real sentence, and most guys never say it out loud because it sounds soft. Say it anyway, even if it feels soft to say out loud.

He's probably thinking the same thing and waiting for you to go first. The friendship doesn't have to end just because the reason you started it did, not if somebody actually picks up the phone and builds a new reason before the old one finishes disappearing for good.
