Three weeks into softball season, one parent has driven to every practice, signed the concussion form, texted the coach about the rainout, and iced a scraped knee twice. The other parent has been to two games.

Nobody voted on this. It happened the way these things happen: whoever registered the kid in March became the parent who runs the season in September.

## The four jobs

A youth sports season is not one job. It is four.

Driving and scheduling. Paperwork and coach communication. The emotional labor of debriefs after a bad game and pep talks before a big one. And presence: who is actually standing on the sideline, week after week.

Most families never split these on purpose. One parent absorbs all four because that parent was free the week sign-ups opened, or naturally organized, or just around the house more that season. By October the arrangement looks permanent. Nobody chose it. It just set.

## Why the default sticks

The default parent keeps doing it because stopping feels riskier than continuing. Handing off the team group chat mid-season looks like dropping the ball. Asking your spouse to take Tuesday pickup feels like admitting you can't handle it.

So the imbalance calcifies. The default parent gets quietly worn down. The other parent doesn't see it, because from where they're standing, the season is running fine. It is. That's the problem.

## Name it before the season, not during it

Two parents sit down before the season starts and assign each job out loud. Who owns driving. Who owns the coach's texts. Who owns form deadlines. Who's on the sideline Tuesday, and who takes Saturday.

Say the split plainly, even when it ends up uneven. One parent travels for work three weeks a month. The other doesn't. The travel parent takes every form and every check. The parent at home takes every practice. That split isn't equal. It's visible, and a visible unequal split beats a silent one every time.

## When the split doesn't hold

No split survives a real season untouched. A tournament weekend adds four hours of driving nobody accounted for. A work trip lands on the one Saturday you were supposed to cover. Check the split again at the season's midpoint, not just at the start.

Ask one question at that check-in: who's carrying more than they agreed to. Fix it then. Don't wait for May, when the answer has curdled into resentment nobody named on purpose either.

## The part that isn't about logistics

The parent who missed most of the games still needs a way in. A ten-minute call after practice. A text asking what happened at the meet. The kid doesn't need both parents at every game. The kid needs both parents to know what happened at the games they missed.

That's the actual job of co-parenting a season. Not splitting the driving evenly. Making sure neither parent turns into a stranger to what the season was.
