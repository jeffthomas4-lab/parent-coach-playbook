Your kid strikes out three times in a row and cries in the car. That's a bad game.

Your kid strikes out three times in a row and, two weeks later, is still skipping practice, sleeping badly, and telling you the team doesn't need her anyway. That's the line.

A sport psychologist is not for every rough patch. Most kids move through slumps, nerves, and one ugly loss on their own, faster than parents expect.

The signal isn't how bad the moment looked. It's whether the moment is still running the kid's life two or three weeks later, in places that have nothing to do with the sport.

## What warrants the call

Sleep that changed and stayed changed. Appetite that's off. A kid who used to want to go to practice and now finds excuses not to, while friendships outside the team go quiet along with everything else.

A mood that stays low all day, not just after a bad rep. Talk of being good for nothing if the sport goes away. Two weeks of any one of these, and you make the call instead of waiting to see.

Injury recovery gets the same read. A kid who's medically cleared but still flinching, still avoiding contact, still talking about the injury months out like it just happened, is dealing with something a physical therapist can't fix.

## What doesn't

One bad game. Pre-game butterflies. A kid who's quiet in the car because the team lost and fine by dinner.

A slump that annoys the kid but isn't swallowing the rest of the week is the normal cost of caring about something in front of other people. No specialist needed. Just a parent who doesn't make the car ride worse.

## Sport psychologist or pediatrician first?

Start with the pediatrician if there's any question whether this is physical, medical, or anxiety showing up as a stomachache or a headache. A pediatrician rules out other causes and refers you to the right specialist, sport psychologist or general adolescent therapist, depending what's going on. Don't diagnose it yourself from the sideline.

Once the issue is clearly performance or identity, not a broader clinical one, that's when a credentialed sport psychologist or mental performance consultant is the right call. Telling a licensed psychologist from a certified mental performance consultant, and a legitimate one from someone who just bought a domain name, is its own question. The [credentialing breakdown](/body/mental-performance-coach-credentials/) covers that ground so this piece doesn't have to.

## What the first session looks like

Nothing dramatic. A conversation, usually with the kid alone for part of it and the parent looped in at the start and end. Questions about what's happening in the kid's head before, during, and after the moments that are hard.

No lying on a couch, no diagnosis handed down in one meeting. Most families are surprised by how ordinary it feels.

## Raising it with your kid

Skip "I think something's wrong with you." Try "I've noticed practice has been rough lately, and I think talking to someone who works with athletes on exactly this might help."

Name it as a tool serious athletes use, because it is. The framing matters more than the word "psychologist" ever will, and most kids hear the difference.

Most kids who hit a rough patch don't need this. The ones who do usually needed it two or three weeks before a parent finally made the call.
