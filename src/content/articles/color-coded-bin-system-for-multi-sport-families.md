Three kids, three sports, one hallway by the back door. Cleats end up in the goalie bag. A swim cap turns up in the baseball bag two weeks later, dried stiff and forgotten.

## One color per kid, not per sport

Sport-based color systems don't survive a season change. Soccer green works for four months, then the sport changes and the bin is wrong again.

Pick a color per kid instead. Blue is Maya's color whether she's in cleats or a swim cap. Buy her a plastic bin in that color and a roll of matching duct tape, sold at any hardware store in enough shades to cover a five-kid household without repeating.

## Tag the gear, not just the bin

The bin catches loose gear at home. The tape identifies it once that gear leaves the house. Wrap a strip around a water bottle, a mouthguard case, a stick handle, the zipper pull on a bag that looks exactly like six other black bags on the same rack.

Mark the inside of helmets and cleats too, where tape peels off in a wash. A permanent marker on the tag works too.

## Where the bin actually lives

Line the bins up by the door everyone actually uses. A bin in the garage or a closet doesn't get refilled, because nobody walks past it on the way out.

Each bin holds one week: whatever gear is clean and not already packed in the current sport bag. Dirty gear goes straight to the laundry, not back in the bin.

## The system holds up because it stays simple

Skip the labeled sub-dividers and the laminated chart. A kid who can match blue to blue can run this alone by age seven or eight. That's the actual point: less time as the family's lost-and-found department, more mornings out the door on time.
