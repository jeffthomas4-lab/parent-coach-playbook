Fall soccer practice opens the third week of August for most high school programs. The first two weeks of that practice run under a rule most parents have never heard of: the heat-acclimatization period.

Athletic trainers built the rule after tracking when heat deaths actually happen. Almost all of them happen in the first few days of a season, not the hottest days of the month. A kid's body needs time to adjust before it can handle full-intensity practice, and the rule forces that time whether a coach wants to take it or not.

The guideline comes from the National Athletic Trainers' Association's 2009 consensus statement, adopted since by most state athletic associations in some form. It defines the heat-acclimatization period as the first 14 consecutive days of practice, counted from a kid's actual first day on the field, not the calendar. Miss a few days to illness or a scheduled rest day, and those days don't count against the total.

Days one through five carry the strictest limits. One practice a day, capped at three hours. A coach can add a walk-through, but it has to run under an hour, skip pads and sport equipment, and sit at least three hours away from the real practice on the clock.

Soccer skips the equipment ramp-up that football and field hockey go through. Under Maryland's and New Jersey's state policies, both modeled on the national guideline, shin guards and goalkeeper gloves are permitted from day one, since neither piece traps heat the way a football helmet or shoulder pads does.

Day six is when double-practice days can start, and the rule still protects recovery. A double must be followed by a single-practice day or a full rest day, and total time on a double-practice day caps at five hours, no single session past three. Six straight days of practice triggers a mandatory day off.

Ask your program's coach or athletic trainer which state policy governs your league, since the exact hour caps shift by state. What doesn't shift is the principle: the first two weeks are supposed to feel conservative. A program running full-intensity two-a-days in week one isn't ahead of the rule.
