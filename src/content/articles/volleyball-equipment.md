Start with the three things that actually matter: court shoes, knee pads, and a ball sized for your child's age. Everything else, from nets to training carts, comes after those three are right. For most families, that means a youth light ball for players under 12 (per [USAV's simplified youth rules](https://usavolleyball.org/resource/simplified-youth-rules/)), a pair of court shoes with lateral support, and a low-profile kneepad. Brands like Mikasa, Tachikara, and Wilson cover those bases at every price point. Parentcoachdesk's [youth volleyball gear list](/what-to-buy/volleyball/) walks you through exactly what to buy by age so you don't overbuy on the first trip.

**Quick shortlist before you read further:**

- **Youth light ball (U12 and younger):** Mikasa MVA-Lite or Tachikara youth series; lighter materials build contact confidence
- **Indoor club ball (U14 and up):** Molten or Mikasa USAV-approved match ball
- **Court shoes:** Any volleyball-specific shoe with gum rubber sole; biggest performance impact for young players
- **Knee pads:** Low-profile, age-appropriate density; skip the bulky adult versions for kids under 10
- **Adjustable net system:** [Gopher Sport](https://gophersport.com/sports/volleyball?p=10\&srsltid=AfmBOook8qorswEmdYOzCx5Nj5hv0I6YC5ZdvF5vAcGV3x3fqeGjssn5) or Gold Medal portable systems for practice and backyard play
- **Starter team kit:** [BSN SPORTS](https://www.bsnsports.com/c/equipment/sports/volleyball/) bulk orders for school and club programs

## What are the official court, net, and ball standards you need to know?

These are the numbers that govern play in the U.S. Know them before you buy a net or order team balls.

**Court dimensions:** The standard indoor court has specified dimensions per the 2025-2027 USAV Indoor Rules Book. Younger age groups use reduced configurations; USAV's simplified youth rules allow smaller court setups to increase rally success and skill repetition.

**Net heights (standard):**

| Level | Net height |
|---|---|
| Youth (U12 and younger) | Reduced; varies by program |

**Ball specs (official adult indoor):** Circumference 65–67 cm, weight 260–280 g, pressure 4.26–4.61 psi. Youth light balls use the same circumference but lighter materials.

> **Stat callout:** The USAV Indoor Rules Book sets official ball pressure at **4.26–4.61 psi** for adult indoor play. A ball outside that range plays differently enough to affect serve and pass mechanics.

**Uniform basics:** USAV uniform requirements mandate permanent Arabic numerals 1–99, with a minimum heights on the front and back to ensure visibility. Liberos must wear a jersey with a clearly contrasting dominant color from their teammates. When in doubt, test your jersey combinations in grayscale before ordering; referees make the final call on marginal contrast cases, so having a backup jersey is smart.

## What gear actually helps kids develop, and what can you skip?

Age and physical development matter more than birth year when picking gear. A tall, strong 10-year-old may be ready for a standard youth ball; a smaller 12-year-old might still benefit from a lighter version. Use the table below as a starting point, then adjust based on your child's comfort with the ball.

| Stage | Ball type | Net height | Footwear | Knee pads |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| — | Super-light (foam or trainer ball) | Lowered significantly | Supportive sneaker | Optional |
| U10 | Youth light ball | Reduced | Volleyball or cross-trainer | Low-profile |
| U12 | Youth light ball | Reduced (USAV youth rules) | Volleyball shoe | Low-profile |
| U14 | Standard youth or adult | Standard women's height | Volleyball shoe | Standard |
| Teen/club | USAV-approved adult ball | Standard by gender | Volleyball shoe | Position-appropriate |

[Stage-appropriate programs](https://volleyball.ca/uploads/About/LTAD/How_it_works_Stage_Appropriate_Volleyball_June_21_EN.pdf) consistently show that matching equipment to a child's development stage, rather than their age alone, produces better skill retention and more enjoyment.

**Before any purchase, confirm:**

- Shoe fits with a thumb's width at the toe (kids' feet grow fast mid-season)
- Knee pad sits centered on the kneecap without sliding down during a squat
- Ball feels comfortable to hold with both hands; child can serve it overhand without wincing

**What to skip on a first purchase:** Ball carts, spike trainers, and rebounder nets are useful training tools, but only once a child is practicing multiple times per week with a coach. Buying them early usually means they sit in a garage.

## How do you keep gear safe and match-ready all season?

A five-minute check before every practice prevents most mid-season surprises.

**Ball pressure:** Use a needle pump with a built-in gauge. Check pressure before each session; balls lose air gradually, especially in cold gyms. The SCSN equipment guidelines recommend using a pressure gauge; USAV sets official indoor ball pressure at 4.26–4.61 psi for match play. Store balls at room temperature; cold storage drops pressure and stiffens the shell.

**Net and post checks:** Before practice, run your hand along the net tape to feel for fraying. Check that post padding is secured and covers the full post height required by your facility. Exposed cables or loose padding are the most common net-related safety hazards at the youth level.

**Footwear and knee pads:** Replace court shoes when the gum rubber sole shows visible wear patterns or when your child reports ankle slipping. Knee pads lose their padding density after heavy use; a pad that slides down during play has lost its structural integrity and should be replaced. Wash knee pads weekly in a mesh laundry bag to prevent skin irritation.

**Safety note:** USAV rules prohibit players from wearing hard casts during play. Soft braces and approved ankle supports are generally permitted, but always confirm with your league's specific rules before a tournament.

**Pro Tip:** *Keep a small emergency kit in your coaching bag: a needle pump, a spare pair of knee pads in the most common size on your roster, and a roll of athletic tape. You'll use all three before the season ends.*

## Where should you buy volleyball gear in the U.S.?

The right channel depends on whether you're buying for one player or an entire team.

**For individual players:** All Volleyball offers strong buyer-advice content alongside its product selection, which makes it useful for parents who want guidance alongside the purchase. Wilson and Tachikara balls are also widely available at Dick's Sporting Goods and Target for parents who want to see the product before buying.

**For school and club programs:** BSN SPORTS is the go-to for bulk team orders, catalog-level selection, and team kit packages. Gopher Sport specializes in nets, training aids, and portable systems, which makes it the better call when you're outfitting a gym or a multi-court practice space.

**For facility-grade net systems:** Gold Medal's competition-quality systems are built for permanent or semi-permanent installation. They're not portable, but they're the right call for a gym that runs tournaments.

> **Before placing a bulk order, ask three questions:** What is the return window if a size is wrong? Does the warranty cover manufacturer defects, and for how long? Are the net dimensions compatible with your existing post spacing? Getting those answers in writing before the order ships saves significant headaches.

When timing matters (a tournament is two weeks out), order online with express shipping or buy locally. Shipping windows for large net systems can run 7–14 business days, and a delayed net is a real problem for a scheduled practice.

- Knee pads

- Backup knee pads

**Bag organization tip:** Use one compartment for shoes only (keeps the rest of the bag clean), one for recovery and medical items, and the main compartment for apparel and the ball. Players who can find their gear in under 30 seconds before warm-up arrive mentally ready.

## How do you maintain the right ball pressure all season?

Ball pressure is the most overlooked part of gear maintenance, and it directly affects how the ball plays. USAV sets the official indoor ball pressure at 4.26–4.61 psi; a ball inflated above this range feels rock-hard and punishes young passers' forearms. One below this range loses its shape on contact and makes serving inconsistent.

Check pressure with a dial gauge, not just by squeezing the ball. Needle pumps with built-in gauges cost under $20 at most sporting goods stores and are worth every cent. Inflate slowly and check the reading after each pump stroke rather than inflating all at once.

**Storage matters too.** Balls stored in a cold car trunk or outdoor shed lose pressure faster. Bring balls inside after practice. If you're storing a ball for more than a few weeks, deflate it slightly (to around 4 psi) to reduce stress on the bladder, then re-inflate before use.

## Can you customize volleyball gear with team colors and logos?

Yes, and it's more accessible than most parents expect. BSN SPORTS offers team customization on jerseys, shorts, and warm-up gear, including logo placement and color matching for club and school programs. Under Armour's team division handles custom apparel for programs that want technical fabric with branded design.

For balls, Tachikara and Wilson both offer custom-printed options for teams and events, typically with a minimum order quantity. Molten and Mikasa custom orders are available through authorized distributors, though lead times can run four to six weeks, so plan ahead of your season start.

One practical note: custom jerseys must still meet USAV uniform requirements for number size, placement, and libero color contrast. Run your design past your league coordinator before placing a large order.

## What warranty and return policies should you expect?

Policies vary significantly by brand and retailer, so read the fine print before buying.

Most major ball brands (Mikasa, Molten, Tachikara) cover manufacturing defects for one year from purchase. That covers seam separation and bladder failure, but not normal wear or punctures. Keep your receipt.

Net systems from Gold Medal and Gopher Sport typically carry longer structural warranties (often one to three years on frames), but padding and net fabric are usually considered consumables and are not covered. Ask specifically about what is and isn't included.

For online purchases, All Volleyball and BSN SPORTS both publish return windows on their sites; check those before ordering, especially for shoes and knee pads where fit is critical. Buying shoes locally first, then ordering online once you know the size, is a practical way to avoid return shipping costs.

## How do the major brands compare on quality and price?

Mikasa and Molten sit at the top for competition quality. Both produce USAV and FIVB-approved balls used at the highest levels of play, and both offer youth versions that maintain that quality at a lower price point. Molten's distinctive panel design is a genuine performance feature, not just aesthetics; the panels affect how the ball rotates and how passers read its trajectory.

Tachikara earns its place through durability across surfaces. It's the ball you hand a beginner or use for outdoor practice without worrying about it. Wilson occupies a similar space but with even broader retail availability, which matters when you need a replacement ball the day before a tournament.

BSN SPORTS and Gopher Sport are not ball brands; they're the infrastructure layer. BSN SPORTS is where a school athletic director goes to outfit an entire program. Gopher Sport is where a parent-coach goes to find a portable net system that actually fits in a minivan.

Under Armour's volleyball apparel is genuinely technical. Moisture-wicking fabrics and compression fits matter more in a long tournament than in a single practice, and the price reflects that. For everyday practice, a basic athletic shirt works fine.

## What most parents get wrong about buying volleyball gear

Here's the honest version: most families overbuy on the first trip and underbuy on the things that actually protect their kid.

A $120 training rebounder sitting unused in a garage is a story every volleyball parent knows. The child played twice, lost interest in solo training, and the rebounder became a very expensive coat rack. Meanwhile, the same family bought a $15 pair of knee pads that slid down every rally because they were sized for an adult.

The gear that earns its money is unglamorous: a well-fitted shoe, a pad that stays put, a ball that doesn't hurt to pass. Those three things, chosen correctly for the child's age and size, do more for skill development and enjoyment than any training gadget.

Budget is real, and nobody should feel pressured to spend more than their situation allows. But if you're going to spend carefully, spend on safety and fit first. The [youth sports cost question](/drive-there/are-youth-sports-worth-it/) is one worth thinking through before a season starts, and Parentcoachdesk has a clear-eyed resource for exactly that conversation.

One more thing: gear does not replace coaching or repetition. A child with a proper ball, good shoes, and a parent who shows up consistently will develop faster than one with a full equipment catalog and no one to practice with.
