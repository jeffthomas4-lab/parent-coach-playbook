Start with the three things that matter: court shoes, knee pads, and a ball sized for your child's age. Everything else, from nets to training carts, comes after those are right.

For most families, that means a light ball for players under 12, which [USAV's simplified youth rules](https://usavolleyball.org/resource/simplified-youth-rules/) name as the official youth ball, plus court shoes with lateral support and a low-profile kneepad. Mikasa, Tachikara, and Wilson all make options at different price points, and our [youth volleyball gear list](/what-to-buy/volleyball/) walks you through exactly what to buy by age so you don't overbuy on the first trip.

**Quick shortlist before you read further:**

- **Youth light ball (U12 and younger):** a [youth light volleyball](/go/volleyball-ball-youth-light/) or the [Mikasa Volley Lite](/go/volleyball-volley-lite/); lighter materials make contact easier
- **Indoor club ball (U14 and up):** a USAV-approved match ball once the lighter ball stops challenging them
- **Court shoes:** a [volleyball court shoe](/go/volleyball-shoes-upcourt/) with a gum rubber sole; the purchase that matters most
- **Knee pads:** [low-profile youth knee pads](/go/volleyball-knee-pads-youth/), age-appropriate density; skip the bulky adult versions for kids under 10
- **Adjustable net system:** a [portable net system](/go/volleyball-net/), or [Gopher Sport](https://gophersport.com/sports/volleyball?p=10\&srsltid=AfmBOook8qorswEmdYOzCx5Nj5hv0I6YC5ZdvF5vAcGV3x3fqeGjssn5) portable systems, for practice and backyard play
- **Starter team kit:** [BSN SPORTS](https://www.bsnsports.com/c/equipment/sports/volleyball/) bulk orders for school and club programs

## What are the official court, net, and ball standards you need to know?

Know these numbers before you buy a net or order team balls.

**Court dimensions:** The adult indoor court is 18 by 9 meters under the 2025-2027 USAV Indoor Rules Book. USAV's simplified youth rules call for smaller courts, often 4 meters wide and 4.5 to 6 meters deep, so rallies last longer and kids get more touches.

**Net heights (standard):**

| Level | Net height |
|---|---|
| Younger youth players | 5 to 6 feet |
| 12 and under | About 7 feet |
| Girls' and women's indoor | 2.24 m (7 ft 4 1/8 in) |
| Boys' and men's indoor | 2.43 m (7 ft 11 5/8 in) |

**Ball specs (official adult indoor):** Circumference 65 to 67 cm, weight 260 to 280 g, pressure 4.26 to 4.61 psi. Youth light balls are adult-size balls made of lighter materials.

> **Stat callout:** The USAV Indoor Rules Book sets official ball pressure at **4.26 to 4.61 psi** for adult indoor play. Outside that range, a ball plays differently enough to throw off serves and passes.

**Uniform basics:** USAV uniform requirements call for numbers 1 to 99, with minimum heights on the front and back. Liberos wear a jersey in a clearly contrasting color from their teammates.

Test jersey combinations in grayscale before ordering. Referees make the final call on close contrast cases, so a backup jersey is smart.

## What gear actually helps kids develop, and what can you skip?

Physical development matters as much as birth year when picking gear. A tall, strong 10-year-old may outgrow the light ball early, while a smaller 12-year-old still needs it.

Use the table as a starting point, then adjust to your child's comfort with the ball.

| Stage | Ball type | Net height | Footwear | Knee pads |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First-timers (about 3 to 7) | Super-light (foam, beach, or trainer ball) | 5 to 6 feet | Supportive sneaker | Optional |
| U10 | Youth light ball | 5 to 6 feet, rising toward 7 | Volleyball or cross-trainer | Low-profile |
| U12 | Youth light ball | About 7 feet (USAV youth rules) | Volleyball shoe | Low-profile |
| U14 | Adult-weight ball | Varies by league | Volleyball shoe | Standard |
| Teen/club | USAV-approved adult ball | Standard by gender | Volleyball shoe | Position-appropriate |

Volleyball Canada's [stage-appropriate volleyball model](https://volleyball.ca/uploads/About/LTAD/How_it_works_Stage_Appropriate_Volleyball_June_21_EN.pdf) builds the same idea into its programs: match equipment and court size to a child's development stage.

**Before any purchase, confirm:**

- Shoe fits with a thumb's width at the toe (kids' feet grow fast mid-season)
- Knee pad sits centered on the kneecap without sliding down during a squat
- Ball feels comfortable to hold with both hands; child can serve it overhand without wincing

**What to skip on a first purchase:** Ball carts, spike trainers, and rebounder nets are useful training tools, but only once a child is practicing multiple times per week with a coach. Buy them early and they sit in a garage.

## How do you keep gear safe and match-ready all season?

A five-minute check before practice heads off surprises.

**Ball pressure:** Use a needle pump with a built-in gauge and check before each session, since balls lose air gradually. Match play calls for 4.26 to 4.61 psi, the official indoor range, so a quick reading before warm-ups tells you whether the ball is ready.

Store balls at room temperature, because cold drops pressure and stiffens the shell.

**Net and post checks:** Run your hand along the net tape to feel for fraying. Make sure post padding is secured and covers the full post, since exposed cables and loose padding are real hazards around a youth net.

**Footwear and knee pads:** Worn soles mean new shoes. A knee pad that slides down during play has lost its shape and needs replacing.

Wash them weekly in a mesh bag.

**Safety note:** USAV's simplified youth rules say a player with a hard cast should not play, even if it's padded. Confirm your league's rules on braces and supports before a tournament.

**Pro Tip:** *Keep a small emergency kit in your coaching bag: a needle pump, a spare pair of knee pads in the most common size on your roster, and a roll of athletic tape. You'll use all three before the season ends.*

## Where should you buy volleyball gear in the U.S.?

The right channel depends on whether you're buying for one player or an entire team.

**For individual players:** All Volleyball pairs its product selection with buyer guides, which helps parents who want advice with the purchase. Wilson and Tachikara balls are easy to find at big sporting goods stores if you want to see one before buying.

**For school and club programs:** BSN SPORTS is the go-to for bulk team orders, catalog-level selection, and team kit packages. Gopher Sport specializes in nets, training aids, and portable systems, which makes it the better call when you're outfitting a gym or a multi-court practice space.

**For facility-grade net systems:** Gold Medal builds competition systems for permanent installation. They don't travel, but they're the right call for a gym that runs tournaments.

> **Before placing a bulk order, ask three questions:** What is the return window if a size is wrong? Does the warranty cover manufacturer defects, and for how long? Are the net dimensions compatible with your existing post spacing? Get the answers in writing before the order ships.

When timing matters, buy locally or pay for faster shipping. Large net systems can take a while to arrive, so order well before the first scheduled practice.

## How do you maintain the right ball pressure all season?

Ball pressure is the most overlooked part of gear care, and it changes how the ball plays. Above the official 4.26 to 4.61 psi range, a ball feels rock-hard and punishes young passers' forearms, and below it the ball goes soft and serves get inconsistent.

Check with a gauge, not a squeeze. A needle pump with a built-in gauge is cheap and worth it, and inflating a stroke at a time keeps you from overshooting.

**Storage matters too.** Balls left in a cold trunk or shed lose pressure faster, so bring them inside after practice. For storage longer than a few weeks, let a little air out and re-inflate before use.

## Can you customize volleyball gear with team colors and logos?

Yes, and it's easier than most parents expect. BSN SPORTS offers team customization on jerseys, shorts, and warm-ups for club and school programs, and Under Armour's team division handles custom technical apparel.

Custom-printed balls exist too, usually with a minimum order and a lead time, so ask about both before you promise them to the team for opening day.

Custom jerseys still have to meet USAV rules. Run your design past your league coordinator before you place a large order with any supplier.

## What warranty and return policies should you expect?

Policies vary by brand and retailer, so read the fine print. Ball warranties cover manufacturing defects like seam separation, not normal wear or punctures, so keep your receipt.

For net systems, ask separately about the frame, the padding, and the net fabric. Coverage on wear parts is often thinner than on the frame.

For online orders, check the return window before you buy, especially for shoes and knee pads where fit matters. Trying shoes on locally, then ordering online once you know the size, saves return shipping.

## How do the major brands compare on quality and price?

Mikasa and Molten sit at the top for competition quality. Both make approved match balls used at high levels of play, and both offer youth versions.

Tachikara earns its place through durability. It's the ball you hand a beginner without worrying, and Wilson fills a similar role with broad retail availability, which helps when you need a replacement the day before a tournament.

BSN SPORTS and Gopher Sport aren't ball brands. BSN SPORTS is where a school athletic director outfits a whole program, and Gopher Sport is where a parent-coach finds a portable net system that fits in a minivan.

Under Armour's volleyball apparel is technical, and the price reflects it. Moisture-wicking fabric matters more over a long tournament day than at a single practice, where a basic athletic shirt works fine.

## What most parents get wrong about buying volleyball gear

Most families overbuy on the first trip and underbuy on the things that protect their kid.

Picture a $120 training rebounder sitting unused in a garage. The child used it twice, lost interest in solo training, and it became an expensive coat rack.

Meanwhile, the same family bought cheap knee pads sized for an adult that slid down every rally.

The gear that earns its money is unglamorous: a well-fitted shoe, a pad that stays put, a ball that doesn't hurt to pass. Those three things, chosen correctly for the child's age and size, do more for skill development and enjoyment than any training gadget.

Budget is real, and nobody should feel pushed to spend more than their situation allows. Spend on safety and fit first, and think through the [youth sports cost question](/drive-there/are-youth-sports-worth-it/) before the season starts.

One more thing: gear does not replace coaching or repetition. A child with a proper ball, good shoes, and a parent who shows up consistently will develop faster than one with a full equipment catalog and no one to practice with.
