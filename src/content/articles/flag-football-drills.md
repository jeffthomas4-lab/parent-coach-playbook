## Top Flag Football Drills at a Glance

You don't need forty drills. You need eight or nine that hit every core skill, plus a couple of small games that keep kids from tuning out.

- **Two-line passing warm-up:** two lines 10 to 15 feet apart throw and catch on the move. Setup: [cones](/go/agility-cones-set/) for lines and one [ball](/go/football-rubber-youth/) per pair. 5 minutes, 15 to 20 reps each. Ages 5 to 7 start at 8 feet with underhand tosses, and ages 11 to 13 add a step-and-throw.
- **Gauntlet flag pull:** a ball carrier runs a 15-yard lane while defenders pull one at a time. Setup: two cone lines. 8 minutes. Younger kids walk it first, and older kids go full speed.
- **Route tree relay:** receivers run a slant, a post, and a flag route against air, then against a defender. Ages 8 to 10 run two routes, and ages 11 to 13 run the full tree.
- **Cone shuttle cuts:** sprint to a cone, plant, and cut hard to a second cone. Three cones in an L, 4 reps each.
- **Catch-and-score small game:** three on three, no rush, first team to five end-zone catches wins.
- **Mirror coverage:** a defender shadows a receiver's side-to-side movement without touching.
- **Circle handoffs:** players hand off on the move in a circle, calling "ball" each time.
- **One-on-one contain:** a defender stays square to a ball carrier in a 10-yard box instead of chasing.

**Pro Tip:** *Track clean flag pulls per rep, not just scrimmage results. A kid pulling 3 of 5 is improving even when the team loses.*

## Offense Drills: Passing, Catching, Routes, and Ball Handling

Offense comes down to timing and hands. Build both with a repeatable progression.

1. **Passing by distance.** 8 feet for ages 5 to 7, 12 to 15 feet for 8 to 10, and 20 feet or more with a shoulder turn for 11 to 13.
2. **Routes with footwork cues.** Teach the stem, running straight at the defender, before the break, and call "plant, cut, look" during reps.
3. **Catching progressions.** Chest-high tosses first, then high balls, low balls, and catches with a defender's hand in the lane. Thumbs together above the chest, pinkies together below it.
4. **Ball security.** Carry the ball high and tight, and switch it away from a chasing defender.

## Defense Drills: Flag-Pulling, Pursuit, and Coverage

Missed flags come from bad hand placement and poor angles more than from effort. Fix both in stages.

- **Stationary pull.** Start at arm's length from a standing carrier and practice a downward grab, not a slap.
- **Controlled chase.** The carrier jogs a straight line while the defender closes and pulls at three-quarter speed.
- **Full-speed angle pursuit.** The carrier runs at game speed, and the defender takes the right angle instead of chasing from behind.
- **Gauntlet and box drills.** Several defenders line a lane or box, so hands and angles get tested under pressure.
- **Mirror and shadow drills.** A defender mirrors a receiver's cuts without contact.

The NFL FLAG [drill and video library](https://nflflag.com/football-drills) shows these on video. An NFL club's youth series also demonstrates [flag-pulling technique](https://www.neworleanssaints.com/video/flag-football-drills-2025-pulling-flags) if you'd rather show than tell.

## Warm-Up and Conditioning Drills for Young Players

Warm-ups matter more than most parent-coaches assume, so move from mobility to activation to speed every time, before anyone sprints.

1. **Dynamic mobility.** Leg swings, walking lunges, and ankle circles for 3 minutes.
2. **Activation.** High knees, butt kicks, and side shuffles for 2 minutes.
3. **Build-up runs.** Three sprints at about half, three-quarter, and near-full effort.
4. **Stop work.** Short 10 to 15-yard sprints that end in a controlled stop.
5. **Cuts.** A 45-degree cut at the end of a sprint, the same move receivers and defenders make in games.

[Children's Hospital of Philadelphia](https://www.chop.edu/news/flag-football-growing-fast-benefits-safety-and-common-injuries-kids-and-teens) recommends starting with dynamic stretches and movement-based warm-ups. CHOP also points out that flag football asks kids to accelerate, decelerate, pivot, and cut constantly, and that learning those mechanics helps lower injury risk.

## Sample Practice Session Plans by Time and Age

Copy one of these into your notes. Each runs warm-up, stations, small game, cool-down, with fewer reps for ages 5 to 7 and more for 11 to 13.

- **30 minutes:** 5-minute warm-up, two 10-minute stations (passing and flag pull), 10-minute small game, 5-minute stretch and water.
- **45 minutes:** 8-minute warm-up, three 10-minute stations (routes, flag pull, ball handling), 12-minute scrimmage, 5-minute cool-down.
- **60 minutes:** 10-minute warm-up, four 10-minute stations, 15 minutes of small games, 5-minute cool-down and recap.

With two or three parent helpers, run one station per adult and rotate every 8 to 12 minutes. Post a clear [league code of conduct](https://www.flexleagueplus.com/blog/league-code-of-conduct) too.

| Age group | Reps per drill | Station ratio | Scrimmage time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5–7 years | 3–4 reps | 1 coach per 5 players | 5–10 minutes |
| 8–10 years | 5–6 reps | 1 coach per 6–8 players | 10–12 minutes |
| 11–13 years | 6–8 reps | 1 coach per 8–10 players | 12–15 minutes |

## Coaching Progressions: What to Teach First

Teach footwork first, then catching, then flag technique, and only then the decisions that tie it all together. Kids who can't plant and cut yet just get frustrated by decision drills.

Small games teach better than endless reps. A three-on-three catch-and-score game makes a receiver read coverage in real time, and the same game-first approach runs through [game-activity coaching methods](https://www.usyouthsoccer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/160/2023/09/Official-US-Youth-Soccer-Coaching-Manual-1.pdf) in other youth sports.

**Pro Tip:** *Keep mid-drill feedback to a few words: "Eyes up." "Hands ready." "Plant foot."*

## Safety Checklist Before Every Practice

- Run the full dynamic warm-up before any sprinting or cutting.
- Give water breaks before, during, and after practice, especially in heat.
- Require [cleats or turf shoes](/go/multi-sport-cleats-youth/) with real traction, since sneakers slip on wet grass.
- Stop or change a drill when a player reports pain that lingers or gets worse.
- Skip tackling drills entirely. In a CDC study, tackle players ages 6 to 14 took [15 times more head impacts](https://www.cdc.gov/traumatic-brain-injury/data-research/comparing-head-impacts/index.html) than flag players during a practice or game.
- Cap full-speed pursuit reps and use [USA Football's practice guidelines](https://usafootball.com/coaches-organizations/practice-guidelines) to keep intensity right for the age.

## What Youth Flag Coaches Get Wrong About Drills

Most practice plans are built backward. Coaches pile on repetition drills because they're easy to run, then wonder why kids check out by week three, when a catch-and-score game teaches more decision-making in ten minutes.

Flag pulling gets treated as an afterthought. It decides more plays than almost anything else, and the stepwise progression from stationary to full-speed fixes more missed flags than extra scrimmage time.

Keep the order: footwork, catching, flag technique, decisions. Rush it and nine-year-olds look lost in week one and still look lost in week six.
