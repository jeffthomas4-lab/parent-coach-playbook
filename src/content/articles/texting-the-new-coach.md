The team email lands with the new head coach's number. The temptation is a thoughtful introduction text covering your kid's history, what worked last season, what didn't, and how you hope this one goes.

Don't. Send two sentences.

## The long text and the short one

Here's the long version, the one that feels helpful when you write it:

> "Hi Coach, this is (your name), (your kid)'s dad. Just wanted to introduce myself before the season. She loved playing for her coach last year and we're excited. Quick background: she's mostly a midfielder but played some forward and is comfortable in either spot. She had some confidence issues midseason after missing a couple of breakaways but came back strong. Anyway, looking forward to meeting you, let us know what we can do to help!"

And the short one:

> "Hi Coach, this is (your name), (your kid)'s dad. Looking forward to the season. Let us know if you need anything from us."

## Why the short one wins

It says who you are. It offers help without managing anything, and it leaves your kid's story for the coach to see in person.

The long text plants three seeds you didn't mean to plant. The coach now wonders about her position, carries a confidence story into the first practice, and has you filed as a parent who manages by text.

If the coach wants history, they'll ask the old coach or watch the first practice. Both are better sources than your summary.

## What to save for later

Some things do need a conversation, like an allergy the coach must know about or a standing schedule conflict. Ask for five minutes at the [preseason meeting with the new coach](/drive-there/pre-season-meeting-with-the-new-coach/) and handle it there.

Keep the hard stuff out of text entirely. [The text to the coach you should never send](/drive-home/text-to-the-coach-you-should-never-send/) shows why, and [how to talk to your kid's coach](/team-parent/how-to-talk-to-the-coach/) covers the in-person version.

Send the two sentences. Then let the coach meet your kid at practice.
