Every cross country season starts with the same five minutes, and almost nobody explains it before it happens.

Sometime in the first week of official practice in August, the coach lines the team up on the school track or a flat stretch of the course and times a mile, sometimes a mile and a half. No pageantry, no announcement that this is the moment, just a start line, a stopwatch, and a list of times posted on a clipboard by the gym door that afternoon.

That list becomes the first varsity picture of the season. NFHS rules cap a varsity race at 7 scoring-eligible runners per school, and most programs seed those 7 spots with whoever ran fastest at that first trial. A kid who spent June and July logging summer miles usually shows up on that list; a kid who skipped the voluntary runs usually doesn't.

**What the trial actually looks like.** Most programs time a mile on the track, or run it as an out-and-back on the course. No bib number, no starting gun, just a coach with a stopwatch and an assistant calling out times as kids cross a chalk line. Results usually post the same day, not the same week, so check the team app that afternoon instead of waiting.

**Why the first one hits harder than the ones in October.** By October, everyone on the team has raced enough times to know roughly where they stand. In August, this is the only number anyone has. A freshman who has never raced a cross country mile in her life gets ranked against seniors who have run it fifty times, on day three of practice, before she has met most of her teammates, and that gap between new to the sport and ranked against the whole roster is what makes the first trial feel bigger than it is.

**The first trial is not a final answer.** Most programs re-run the trial on a rolling basis through the season, and a kid who is 12th in August can be scoring varsity points by October if the training sticks. We've written about [how a coach actually picks the varsity 7](/drive-there/varsity-cross-country-the-real-odds/) in more detail; the short version is that nothing in August is permanent. Tell your kid that before the first list goes up, not after they've already decided what it means about them.

**What actually predicts a good August time trial.** Summer mileage predicts it, not natural speed. A kid who ran three easy miles a few mornings a week in June and July almost always outruns a naturally faster kid who did none of it. Coaches know this, which is why the good ones frame the trial as a snapshot of summer effort, not a talent test.

**The car ride question that helps.** Skip "where did you place." Ask "did you run it the way you wanted to" instead. A time trial rewards pacing as much as fitness, and a kid who went out too fast and faded has a different problem than a kid who simply isn't fit yet.

Both are fixable. Neither gets fixed by staring at a clipboard in August.

**If your kid skipped summer running.** One bad August time trial does not doom a season. A kid who starts training seriously in week one instead of week negative-six is behind the roster, not behind for good, and coaches who have run the sport for a while have seen plenty of JV freshmen turn into varsity juniors on nothing but consistent mileage.
