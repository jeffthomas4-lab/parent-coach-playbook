A mom at a swim meet asked us last month if her family was ruining their daughter by paying $180 an hour for a private stroke coach twice a week. Another dad asked us the opposite question about his son's baseball team: was he ruining the kid by refusing to pay for it at all.

Both were really asking the same thing. They wanted someone outside the family to tell them they'd made the right call. Nobody outside the family gets to make that call, and nobody outside the family has enough information to grade it after the fact.

## Specialize early or stay multi-sport

One kid plays club volleyball twelve months a year starting at ten. Another plays soccer in the fall, basketball in the winter, and track in the spring, all the way through eighth grade.

The specialization case is real: skill acquisition research backs early, focused repetition for a narrow set of sports like gymnastics and figure skating, and a kid who wants to compete at a high level in those sports by fifteen often needs the hours. The multi-sport case is also real: an NFHS-cited study found high school athletes who specialize in one sport are 70% more likely to get hurt during a season than kids who play multiple sports, and the American Orthopaedic Society for Sports Medicine flags year-round single-sport training before age twelve as the clearest overuse-injury risk factor it tracks.

Both of those facts are true at the same time. Which one matters more for your kid depends on the sport, the kid's body, and what "high level" even means for your family. We're not going to pretend there's a clean answer sitting underneath both of those facts.

## Pay for private coaching or don't

A private pitching lesson runs $60 to $140 an hour depending on the coach and the market, per multiple lesson-booking sites tracking 2026 rates. A season of weekly goalkeeper training can run into four figures. None of it is required to play.

Money spent on private coaching can close a real skill gap fast, and for a kid chasing a roster spot or a scholarship, that gap sometimes matters more than anything a rec-league practice can teach. Money not spent on private coaching stays in the family budget for the things private coaching can't buy: a second kid's activities, a summer that isn't built entirely around one sport, a parent who isn't working a second shift to cover it.

Neither choice says anything about how much you love your kid. A family with the money and the time can skip it, and a family without either can find it anyway. Same decision, two directions, and neither needs defending to us.

## Push through or let them quit

A ten-year-old wants to quit gymnastics in week three of a season she begged to join in June. A fifteen-year-old wants to quit a varsity team two games before senior night, after three years on the roster.

Making a kid finish what they started has a real case behind it: commitment is a skill, teams count on their roster, and a lot of kids who wanted to quit in week three were glad by week ten that nobody let them. Letting a miserable kid walk away now has just as real a case: forcing a kid to finish a season they hate can turn a sport they might have come back to later into a sport they never touch again.

The right answer changes with the kid, the age, the reason behind wanting out, and how many times this particular kid has asked to quit something before. We're not handing you a formula for that, because a formula would be lying about how much this depends on the kid actually sitting at your table.

## What we can actually do here

We can lay out the tradeoffs honestly instead of picking a side and dressing it up as universal advice. We can tell you the pitching lesson costs $60 to $140 an hour so you're deciding with real numbers instead of a guess. We can tell you the actual overuse injury rates so the specialization question isn't just a feeling.

What we can't do is sit at your kitchen table and know what your kid needs this season, in this sport, at this age. Neither can anyone else who isn't in your house. Make the call, live with it, and don't let a stranger's opinion carry more weight than your own read on your own kid.
