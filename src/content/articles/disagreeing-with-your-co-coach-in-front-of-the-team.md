Down two runs in the fifth inning, the assistant coach waves the runner home. The head coach, thirty feet away, is yelling stay. The runner slows down between third and home, unsure which coach to believe, and gets tagged out standing still.

Nobody talks about the call after the game. Two weeks later it happens again, different play, same standoff.

## Pick a winner before anyone notices there's a fight

A team survives one wrong call. Two coaches contradicting each other in front of the team is the one it doesn't survive. The moment adults give opposite instructions on the same play, players stop listening to either one and start reading body language instead.

Decide in advance whose call stands when a disagreement happens mid-play. Base running goes to the third-base coach. Substitutions go to the head coach, no matter who thought of the sub first.

Name the split before the season starts, not while a runner stands on third looking confused. Put it in words out loud to each other, once, before day one.

## The fix is a phrase, not a debate

Agree on a plain phrase in advance for the moment you disagree mid-play. Something as simple as "hang on" or "your call" tells your co-coach you saw it differently without saying so out loud in front of ten kids and their parents. Say it quietly, once, and let the standing call hold.

What doesn't happen is public reversal. The assistant does not tell the runner to stop after the head coach already sent her, even when the assistant is right, because a wrong call held together does less damage to the team than a right call delivered as a correction.

## Have the actual conversation later

Every disagreement earns a real conversation, and the parking lot after practice is the place for it, not the water break where three parents can still hear you. Say exactly what you saw and ask the question straight: "I had her going on contact, you had her holding, which one of us reads that better?"

That's a five-minute conversation, not a grudge.

## What repeats is the real problem

One disagreement in a season is normal. Two coaches who read the game differently are not a crisis, they're two people with different instincts. Three of the same disagreement is the crisis, because nobody had the parking-lot conversation the first time.

If you're having the same hang-on moment every week, the problem isn't in-game communication anymore. Nobody defined the roles clearly before the season started, and no quiet phrase fixes an assignment that was never made in the first place.

Players forget the blown call by the next practice. They don't forget watching the two adults in charge argue about who's right while a kid stands on third base deciding for herself what to do.
