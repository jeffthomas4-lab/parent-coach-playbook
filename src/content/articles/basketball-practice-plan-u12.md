Run a 45 to 60 minute practice built in four parts: a 10 minute warm-up and ball-handling block, a 20 to 25 minute focused skill segment, 10 to 12 minutes of stations or small-sided games, and 8 to 10 minutes of controlled scrimmage. This structure matches [USA Basketball's](https://www.usab.com/youthguidelines) guidance on age-appropriate practice length and keeps every segment short enough to hold a nine to eleven year old's attention. Below, we break down the minute-by-minute plan, the drills that fill it, and the safety basics every parent-coach should know.

***

> **TL;DR:**
>
> - Post the written plan, assign adults to skill instruction and station resets, and rotate substitutes every two to three minutes to keep participation equitable.
> - Choose one skill, teach it, let players practice with frequent repetitions, then add defense, a time limit, or a decision only after consistent success.
> - Run stations for three to four minutes, pair players for immediate turn changes, and use 3v3 rules that reward a completed pass.
> - Because two out of three concussions in boys’ basketball involve contact, remove suspected cases immediately; return requires six steps, each cleared by a healthcare provider.

## A Ready-to-Run 45 to 60 Minute Practice Timeline

Print this out or read it aloud to your assistants before the first whistle. Keeping it visible for your volunteer helpers cuts down on confusion and downtime, something [NFHS practice-planning guidance](https://nfhs.org/stories/effective-practice-planning) points to as one of the simplest ways to keep a youth practice moving.

1. **Minutes 0 to 10:** Dynamic warm-up and simultaneous ball-handling work.
2. **Minutes 10 to 30:** Main skill block built around one focus skill, broken into three or four short drills.
3. **Minutes 30 to 42:** Stations and small-sided games that apply the day's skill in game-like decisions.
4. **Minutes 42 to 50:** Controlled scrimmage with rules that reinforce the day's focus.
5. **Minutes 50 to 55:** Cooldown, quick recap, and water.

One head coach and one or two assistant volunteers can run this without a hitch if roles are assigned in advance:

- One adult runs the main skill block and keeps time on each drill.
- A second adult manages station rotations and resets equipment between groups.
- Whoever is free during scrimmage calls stoppages to teach, not just to correct mistakes.

Rotate substitutes every two to three minutes during scrimmage and station work so playing time stays close to equal across the roster. For more printable layouts, our [youth basketball drills guide](/blog/youth-basketball-drills/) has several you can swap into this same timeline.

## Warm-Up and Ball-Handling Work That Keeps Everyone Moving

Skip the single-file layup line. A five minute dynamic warm-up, think high knees, lateral shuffles, and arm circles, gets bodies ready without anyone standing still.

- Partner mirror dribbling: pairs face off, one leads changes of direction while the other mirrors with a ball.
- Dribble knockout variant: everyone dribbles inside a marked area while trying to tap away a neighbor's ball, keeping their own alive.
- Movement dribbling: players dribble while walking, jogging, and backpedaling on a coach's cue.

Coach for head up vision, fingertip control, and low, controlled dribbles. Every player touches a ball the entire time, which is the point.

## The Main Skill Block: One Focus, Three Progressions

Pick one skill per practice, whether that is dribbling, passing, or shooting, and build the block around it instead of cramming in everything at once. USA Basketball's Player Development Curriculum frames this as mastery-based progression: move players forward once they show they can do the skill, not simply because a week has passed.

A simple structure works for any skill:

1. **Teach it:** Demonstrate the skill and the one or two cues that matter most.
2. **Guided practice:** Run a drill where every player gets high repetitions with light coach correction.
3. **Challenge variant:** Add defense, a time limit, or a decision point to raise the difficulty.

Three drills that fit this progression, each in under 12 minutes:

- **Tag dribble:** Players dribble inside a grid while avoiding tags from a designated defender, reinforcing ball control under pressure.
- **Partner passing relay:** Two lines pass back and forth while jogging the length of the court, rewarding accuracy over speed.
- **Close-range shooting circuit:** Small groups rotate through three spots near the basket, focusing on form before distance.

**Pro Tip:** *Let weaker players shoot or dribble from a step closer and stronger players add a defender or a time cap, same drill, different challenge.*

## Stations and Small-Sided Games That Build Game Sense

Stations and small-sided games turn isolated skills into real decisions. Each station should run for three to four minutes before rotating, which keeps energy high and matches the short-segment approach NFHS recommends for youth practices.

- A dribbling station where players weave through cones while scanning for a coach's hand signal.
- A passing station using two-on-one keep-away to force quick decisions.
- A shooting station with a partner rebounding and feeding passes.
- A small-sided 3v3 half-court game where baskets only count after a completed pass, reinforcing teamwork over solo plays.

Pair players so one replaces the other immediately at each station. That single change removes lines almost entirely and roughly doubles how many reps each player gets compared to single-file drills.

## Running a Controlled Scrimmage and Teaching Transition Defense

Scrimmage time should still be teaching time. Limit dribbles to two or three per possession, or only award points after a specific action like a completed extra pass, so the rules reinforce whatever skill the practice focused on.

- Call brief timeouts to correct positioning rather than letting mistakes repeat uncorrected.
- Run short 2-on-1 and 3-on-2 transition drills before scrimmage, emphasizing communication and quick recovery rather than risky gambles for steals.
- Keep scrimmage short, 8 to 10 minutes is plenty for U12, so energy and focus stay high through the final whistle.

## Pacing, Progression, and Simple Ways to Track Growth

Keep every segment under 12 minutes. A practical rule of thumb from NFHS is roughly one minute of active engagement per year of the players' age, meaning a 12 minute cap fits most U12 groups well.

- Rotate to a new activity the moment the clock runs out, even mid-drill.
- Watch for consistent success, not just effort, before introducing a harder variation.
- Jot down two or three quick notes after each practice on who is close to mastering the skill and who needs more reps.

**USA Basketball recommends a a training-to-competition ratio emphasizing more skill work than games for the Foundational Level**, meaning most of a U12 team's weekly hours should go toward skill work and small-sided games rather than full scrimmages or games.

## Keeping Practice Safe: A Quick Concussion Reminder

Collisions happen in basketball, and [CDC Heads Up materials](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/55287) note that two out of three concussions in boys' basketball come from contact with another player. Any suspected concussion means an immediate stop for that athlete.

- Remove the player from practice right away, no exceptions.
- Return to play follows a six-step graduated progression, each step cleared by a healthcare provider before moving to the next.

## Development First, Fun Always

Our honest take: a U12 practice succeeds when every kid gets equal touches, feels a little better at the skill by the final whistle, and wants to come back next week. ## FAQ

### How long should a U12 basketball practice be?

Most U12 practices run between 45 and 75 minutes, according to USA Basketball's youth guidelines. A couple of practices per week is typical for this age group, balanced with some games.

### How many drills should fit into one U12 practice?

Three or four drills built around a single focus skill work well, each lasting under 12 minutes to match typical attention spans at this age, a timing rule NFHS recommends for youth practices. Rotating quickly between drills keeps energy and engagement high.

### What is the right balance between drills and scrimmage time?

USA Basketball's Player Development Curriculum recommends a a training-to-competition ratio emphasizing more skill work than games for the Foundational Level, meaning most practice time should go toward skill work rather than full scrimmages. A short, controlled scrimmage at the end still reinforces the day's lesson.

### What should a coach do if a player might have a concussion?

Remove the player from practice immediately and do not let them return that day. CDC Heads Up materials outline a six-step graduated return-to-play process that requires healthcare provider approval between each step.

### How can parent-coaches keep practices from feeling chaotic?

Posting a written practice plan and assigning specific station roles to assistant volunteers removes a lot of the guesswork, a practice NFHS points to as effective for keeping youth practices organized. Pairing players so one immediately replaces another at each drill also cuts down on lines and standing around.
