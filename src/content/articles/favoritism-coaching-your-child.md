Your kid is stuck on the bench while a teammate with similar skills plays more. That pattern deserves a look, but it isn't automatically favoritism. Today, check the team against its written playing-time policy, or push for one if none exists, and write down what you're seeing before you talk to the coach.

Most disputed lineups trace back to an unclear model not a hidden agenda. Assume nothing yet.

***

> **TL;DR:**
>
> - Lineups usually follow practice effort and team needs, but a pattern of exceptions deserves careful notes.
> - A written policy on playing time, behavior, and complaints gives everyone the same standard.
> - Favoritism shows up as rules applied unevenly, vague reasoning, and a defensive response to questions.
> - Boundary and safety concerns are a different category: act right away and report through SafeSport.
> - Prepare your words and keep records before you raise anything with the coach.

## Quick checklist: 8 immediate steps to manage suspected favoritism

It's hard to think clearly in the middle of it. This order keeps you steady and keeps your kid at the center of every decision you make.

1. Write down what happened: who, when, what was said or done, and who else was there.
2. Find or ask for the team's written policy on playing time, attendance, and conduct.
3. Talk with your kid about how they feel, not about their performance.
4. Ask the coach for a short, private conversation, and bring examples instead of accusations.
5. Skip confrontations at games and stay off social media.
6. If you see boundary or safety concerns, follow [SafeSport reporting guidance](https://uscenterforsafesport.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/Process-Improvement-Announcement-3_28_24-FINAL.pdf) right away.
7. If other parents share the concern, suggest a short team check-in on the policy.
8. Keep emotion out of written messages and ask for a specific follow-up date.

**Pro Tip:** *Keep a three-column log: date, what happened, who saw it. Within a week or two, your feelings turn into facts you can actually show someone.*

## How to tell if what's happening is favoritism, coaching judgment, or perception

Not every disappointing lineup is favoritism. Coaches weigh practice performance, roster needs, and matchups, and some of those calls will go against your kid when nothing unfair is happening at all.

Favoritism shows a pattern. The same kid gets exceptions nobody else gets, the reasoning stays vague, and questions bring a defensive or punitive response.

- One kid gets minutes or roles that don't match recent practice.
- Rules that apply to everyone quietly don't apply to one player.
- The coach won't explain decisions or gets irritated when asked.
- Kids who ask questions see their chances shrink afterward.

Check decisions against the team's stated model. In [USA Volleyball's piece on paying for practice](https://usavolleyball.org/resource/you-are-paying-for-practice-not-playing/), John Kessel says clubs should have written material on mission, philosophy, and expectations, and that most run on either equal time or time earned in practice.

**The fair question isn't whether minutes match.** It's whether the coach follows the model the team stated, and attendance records and practice observations help you check that better than minutes alone.

## Boundary and safety red flags that go beyond favoritism into misconduct

Some behavior isn't about fairness. It's a safety concern, and it calls for faster action than a policy talk.

Watch for one-on-one time with no other adult around, gifts or privileges outside team norms, and messages to a minor that leave parents out. SafeSport's abuse-prevention policies call for observable, interruptible one-on-one contact and for parents to be copied on electronic messages with minor athletes.

- Save messages, note dates and times, and record who else saw the behavior.
- Report through your club's safeguarding lead or through SafeSport.
- If your kid is in immediate danger, remove them and call local authorities.

**Pro Tip:** *Trust the pattern over the single moment. One awkward exchange might be nothing, but three similar ones with the same kid are worth reporting.*

## How to prevent favoritism before it starts with a team policy

Don't let the rules stay unwritten. When the policy is visible before the first practice, an unpopular call reads as a decision, not a secret.

A workable policy fits on one page:

1. A short mission statement for the season.
2. The playing-time model and why you chose it.
3. Consequences for missed practices or games.
4. How and when parents can raise questions.
5. The steps for a complaint, and who handles it.
6. How you'll note the reasoning for close lineup calls.

Different ages need different models. Young, development-focused teams do best with roughly equal time, and older competitive teams often tie minutes to practice effort and skill. A hybrid keeps development first while being upfront about how merit starts to matter.

| Model | Best fit | Strength | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equal time | Youngest development leagues | Every player builds skills and stays engaged | Doesn't reward effort directly |
| Performance based | Older, competitive teams | Rewards practice effort and results | Can widen the gap across the roster |
| Hybrid | Transitional teams | Balances growth with honesty about merit | Needs more communication with parents |

Publish the policy, check in every couple of weeks, and jot down the reasoning behind contested lineups. Ten minutes now saves hours of conflict.

## What to say: scripts for your child, the coach, and other parents

You need words, not just good intentions, for the hard conversations as well as the calm ones.

**With your kid, by age:**

1. Ages 8 to 11: "I noticed you didn't get much time on the court today. How did that feel? What did you enjoy about practice this week?"
2. Ages 12 to 15: "I want to check in about playing time, not to fix it, just to hear how you're feeling."
3. Ages 16 and up: "You're old enough to weigh in. Do you want me to say anything to the coach, or do you want to handle it first?"

**With the coach:**

- "Could we find fifteen minutes? I've noticed a few things and want to understand the reasoning."
- "I wrote down what I saw on these three dates. Can you walk me through how the playing-time model applies?"
- "I'd like clarity on the model and how decisions get made going forward."

If the coach gets defensive, slow down. Ask whether an assistant can join, or suggest the whole team talk through the policy so it isn't about one kid.

**With other parents:** stick to shared outcomes, not rumor. "A few of us have questions about how lineups are decided. Would it help to ask for a short policy meeting?" moves toward a fix instead of a pile-on.

**Pro Tip:** *Say your coach script out loud once before the real talk. It steadies your tone.*

## A parent-coach's short reflection

Coaching your own kid hands you power you didn't fully sign up for. Your kid can't quit the family the way they could quit a club, so fairness weighs more on you than on any other coach.

Over-communicate. Publish the policy when the season looks calm, hold check-ins before anyone complains, and practice the scripts, because the calm version of you reading this is a better communicator than the one in the parking lot after a hard loss.

## FAQ

### How to deal with coach favoritism?

Check decisions against the team's stated playing-time model. If the pattern still looks unfair, write down specific incidents and ask for a private talk built on examples, not accusations.

### What are some signs of parental favoritism in childhood?

One kid gets exceptions to rules everyone else follows. Decisions don't match effort or performance, and questions get a defensive or secretive response, while retaliation after someone raises a concern is one of the clearest signs.

### What to do when a coach is unfair to your child?

Write down specifics, then ask for a short private conversation about those examples and the team's model. Safety issues go straight to SafeSport.

### What is the psychology behind favoritism?

For a parent-coach, the urge to protect your own kid competes with your duty to the whole team. A written model and a few trusted adults who'll tell you the truth help you catch your own bias before it shapes a decision.
