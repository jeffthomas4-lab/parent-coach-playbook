Suspect a concussion? Pull the athlete from play right now, check for danger signs that need the emergency department, and get a healthcare provider's evaluation before they set foot on the field again. No same-day return, no matter how badly the team needs them.

***

> **TL;DR:**
>
> - The CDC HEADS UP protocol has three moves: remove the athlete right away, check for danger signs, and keep them out that day and until a healthcare provider clears them.
> - Return to sport is a stepwise process that takes a week or more, and symptoms that come back send the athlete down a step.
> - Return to school can run alongside return to sport, but kids should be back to normal non-sports life before they finish the sports steps.
> - State laws differ, so ask your league for its written protocol before the season.

## What Is a Concussion Protocol in Youth Sports?

A concussion protocol is the set of steps a team follows from the moment a head injury is suspected until a provider clears the athlete to play. It exists because a second hit before the brain heals is dangerous.

The [CDC's HEADS UP guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/response/index.html) is the base most youth programs borrow from. Remove the athlete right away, check for danger signs, and keep them out the day of the injury and until a healthcare provider clears them. The CDC adds that athletes who return too soon have a greater chance of a repeat concussion.

You don't need a medical degree. You need to know what to watch for, what to do in the first hour, and who to call.

## What Are the Signs of a Concussion in a Young Athlete?

Kids rarely show every symptom. Some show up in minutes and others creep in over a day or two, so "he seemed fine on the sideline" never clears anyone.

**Thinking:**

- Confusion or seeming "in a fog"
- Trouble remembering plays or recent events
- Slowed thinking or trouble concentrating

**Physical:**

- Headache or head pressure
- Nausea or vomiting
- Dizziness or balance problems
- Sensitivity to light or noise
- Blurry vision

**Emotional:**

- Irritability, sadness, or unusual nervousness
- Mood swings that aren't like them

**Sleep:**

- Drowsiness beyond normal tiredness
- Sleeping much more or less than usual
- Trouble falling asleep

Some signs mean the emergency department right away, and CDC HEADS UP lists danger signs including these:

1. One pupil larger than the other
2. Repeated vomiting or nausea
3. A headache that gets worse and won't go away
4. Drowsiness or can't be woken up
5. Weakness, numbness, or decreased coordination
6. Slurred speech, seizures, or convulsions
7. Loss of consciousness, even briefly

See any of those? Call 9-1-1 and let emergency care come first, before you call anyone else on the team.

Without danger signs, the job is still clear. Remove the athlete and keep them out, even for "just one more series," and keep an adult with them. Call the parent right away, then write down the time, how the hit happened, and any loss of consciousness or memory gap.

Don't run your own memory or balance tests to clear them. The CDC is explicit that only a healthcare provider should assess a possible concussion or decide on return. Ask a clinician before giving any medication.

**Pro Tip:** *Keep a laminated danger-signs card on your clipboard. Even parents who know the list cold blank on it in the moment.*

## What Should You Do in the First Hour After a Suspected Concussion?

Screenshot this four-step sequence.

1. **Remove and stay.** Move the athlete to a quiet spot away from the noise and lights, and keep one adult with them the whole time.
2. **Check and record.** Look for danger signs, then write down the time, the cause, any loss of consciousness, any memory gaps, and any past concussions.
3. **Notify and get an evaluation.** Any danger sign means the emergency department. Otherwise, set up a visit with a healthcare provider soon.
4. **Keep the paperwork.** Use a written incident form, because the clinician and later the school will need it.

| Step | Who's responsible | What "done" looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Remove from play | Coach or supervising adult | Off the field and out for the day |
| Check for danger signs | Any trained adult | Checklist reviewed, 9-1-1 called if needed |
| Notify parent | Coach or team manager | Parent has the incident summary |
| Document | Coach or designated recorder | Form done with time, cause, symptoms |

That form isn't busywork. The CDC tells parents to share the cause and force of the hit, any loss of consciousness and how long, any memory loss or seizures, past concussions, and current medications.

## How Does the Six-Step Return-to-Play Progression Work?

Return to sport goes in stages. The CDC calls it a multi-step process that takes a week or more, based on the international consensus on concussion in sport.

**Step 1: Symptom-limited activity.** Normal daily life at a pace that doesn't make symptoms worse.

**Step 2: Light to moderate aerobic exercise.** Walking or a stationary bike.

**Step 3: Individual sport-specific exercise.** Running or skill work alone, away from contact and any risk of a hit to the head.

**Step 4: Non-contact training drills.** Harder team drills with no contact.

**Step 5: Full-contact practice.** Normal contact practice, only after medical clearance from the provider who has been following recovery.

**Step 6: Return to sport.** Full games.

Each step usually takes at least 24 hours. Treat that as a floor, because many clinicians go slower with kids.

Symptoms come back? Stop and drop back a step, or go wherever the clinician says. A setback is the brain asking for more time, not a sign the kid isn't tough.

The CDC says athletes with symptoms lasting more than 2 to 4 weeks should see a specialist experienced in [treating brain injuries](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK448119/). Don't settle for another wait-and-see week.

**Pro Tip:** *A good day isn't a reason to skip a step. Symptoms can show up hours after activity, not just during it, so finish the full step even when your kid feels great.*

## What Does Return to Learn Look Like After a Concussion?

School counts too. Parents fixate on the next game and forget the classroom is where the brain works hardest all day.

School and sport can run together. Kids should be back to all regular non-sports activities, without accommodations, before they finish the sports steps.

Ask the school about adjustments like these:

- Less screen time
- A shorter day or built-in rest breaks
- Extensions on homework and tests
- Extra time or a quieter room for tests
- A lighter homework load at first

If symptoms drag on, ask the school about a formal support plan instead of patching things together teacher by teacher.

Coordinate the two tracks. Ask the clinician to put school and sports recommendations in one written note, so the school and the team work from the same page.

## How Do State Concussion Laws Apply to Club and Recreational Teams?

Every state has a youth concussion law. Most agree on the basics: remove the athlete and require clearance before return.

A [review of state concussion laws](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6378501/) found real differences in who may clear an athlete and whether the law spells out a graduated return. Some states require a physician, while others allow a wider range of licensed providers.

Scope matters most. Many laws were written with school sports in mind, so your kid's school team may be covered while the weekend club team isn't covered the same way.

"The law requires it" is a weak plan for club and travel teams. Ask your league directly:

- Do your coaches use CDC HEADS UP training and materials?
- Which type of provider must clear an athlete to return?
- Do you have a written incident-report policy I can see?
- Is there a written graduated return-to-play plan?

A league that can't answer during registration has told you something.

## What Should a Team's Written Concussion Protocol Include?

Every program needs a written protocol. Unwritten rules fall apart in the chaos of a real injury, with parents watching and a clock running.

**Policy language to put in writing:**

- Any athlete with a suspected concussion comes out right away, no matter the game
- Any danger sign means a 9-1-1 call
- No same-day return, however the athlete looks
- Every incident goes on a standard form shared with parents

**Preseason must-dos:**

- A parent handout on signs, symptoms, and the removal policy
- Free CDC HEADS UP training for every coach
- A written emergency plan naming the nearest emergency department
- Current emergency contacts for every athlete

**On-field readiness:**

| Readiness item | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Assigned incident recorder | Someone besides the coach with the athlete takes notes |
| Working phone with signal | No scrambling if 9-1-1 is needed |
| Known route to nearest ED | Decided before the season |
| Equipment checked preseason | Cuts unrelated injury risk |

Talk with athletes about concussion at the first practice. Teach safe technique and enforce the rules that limit dangerous contact.

Gear plays a supporting role. A properly fitted helmet and [durable, well-maintained gear](https://rnkapparel.com/blogs/rnk-news/why-youth-athletes-need-durable-gear) lower some injury risks, but no equipment prevents every concussion. [Mouth guards](https://clearretain.com/blogs/blog/contact-sport-mouth-guard-benefits-2026-athlete-guide) protect teeth and jaws, so don't sell them to your team as concussion prevention.

## What Should You Ask the Doctor, and How Do You Track Recovery?

Bring your questions in writing. You'll avoid the follow-up call three days later when you remember the one thing you forgot.

1. Do you handle return-to-school planning as well as return to sport?
2. What recovery timeline do you expect from what you see today?
3. Which symptoms should make me call you right away?
4. When would you refer us to a specialist?

Clearance should never be a vague thumbs-up. Ask for a note with the exam date, the activities allowed now, any school accommodations, and a follow-up plan, and if it skips school, ask the provider to add it.

Keep a daily symptom log. Write down the symptoms, the activity level, and a short note every day, even good ones, so the clinician sees a pattern instead of "he seemed better Tuesday."

## A Parent-Coach's Honest Take on Concussion Safety

Parents worry that a strict protocol will cost their kid a season or a roster spot. That fear is real. It still has the risk backwards.

A second concussion before the first one heals is far worse than a missed week. Teams that handle this well treat the protocol as what keeps next season possible.

Talk to your kids about symptoms before anything happens. Tell them saying something feels foggy is not weak, and that hiding it makes the next hit more dangerous.

## FAQ

### How Long Should a Child Wait to Play Sports After a Concussion?

There's no fixed number of days. Return to sport takes a week or more, step by step, with at least 24 hours per step and a provider's clearance before contact.

### What Is the 4-Hour Rule for Concussion?

The CDC HEADS UP response guidance doesn't use that term. If your league uses it, ask what they mean, and follow the provider's clearance either way.

### What Is the Recommended Protocol for a 10-Year-Old With a Concussion?

The core is the same at any age: remove, check for danger signs, get an evaluation, then a stepwise return. Kids often go slower.

### Is There a "20/20/20 Rule" for Concussion Recovery?

The CDC guidance cited here doesn't use that term. Don't let it replace medical clearance and the stepwise return.

### Who Should Clear an Athlete to Return to Play?

A healthcare provider. Which providers qualify depends on your state, so ask your league or school for its policy.
