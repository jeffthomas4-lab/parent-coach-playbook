Your 15-year-old is not starting. They came home frustrated.

The instinct is to advocate: text the coach, argue the lineup, tell them they are better than the starter. Do not do that from the stands.

Ask a better question instead: "Tough spot. What are you going to do about it?" At this age, owning the next action matters more than any parent argument with the coach.

## What game day is for

Watch the game. Cheer when they go in. Stay off your phone's coach thread.

Your kid will feel the energy if you spend four quarters narrating how wrong the coach is. Bitterness is a poor substitute for improvement.

If you need a longer frame for chronic bench roles, read [when your kid is always on the bench](/game/when-your-kid-is-always-on-the-bench/). This page is about one game day.

## Starting jobs and missing feedback

If they are clearly better and ready in the coach's system, playing time usually follows over a season. If they are not playing, the reasons are often skill gaps, readiness, or fit.

Arguing the live lineup rarely changes those. Specific work the coach named can. So can film study, practice habits, or a later roster decision.

If the coach has never told them why they are not starting, that is worth a question. The ask belongs to the athlete: "Can I get feedback on what I need to work on?"

Help them draft it. Do not send it for them unless safety or a documented disability accommodation requires an adult channel.

For the structured conversation later in the week, use [how to talk to the coach about playing time](/drive-there/how-to-talk-to-the-coach-about-playing-time/).

## What you are teaching

At 15-plus you are raising someone who can handle disappointment and decide whether to stay. Bench minutes can teach that if you do not rescue every uncomfortable quarter.

After the ride home, keep the debrief short. Name one controllable next step. Leave the coach's character out of it unless there is a conduct or safety issue that belongs in a formal report.
